# Senior B.Sc. Nurse Interview Questions and Answers

**For:** a B.Sc. Nursing nurse with about 1 to 3 years of experience  
**Interview style:** Indian hospital staff-nurse viva (government or private hospital)  
**Language:** simple English

This note is for interview practice. It is not a hospital protocol. Medicine doses in this note are the answers examiners usually expect. Give a medicine only when a doctor orders it, and follow your own hospital protocol.

When a short form appears for the first time, the full form is written next to it. All short forms are also listed at the end.

---

## How to use this note

Read one topic at a time. Say the answer aloud, as you would in the interview room. Then cover the answer and try the counter questions. A counter question is the next question the examiner asks after hearing your answer. Questions are numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on. Counter questions are numbered under that question, for example 4.1 and 4.2.

A strong clinical answer has five parts:

1. Say the meaning in one sentence.
2. Name the types, or the cause.
3. Say the danger signs.
4. Say what you do first as a nurse.
5. Say what you report to the doctor, and what you write.

If you do not know a dose, do not guess in front of the patient. In the viva you may say, "I will check the protocol and give it only on the doctor's order." Then give the textbook dose if you know it.

Put life-threatening problems first:

1. Not breathing, or the heart has stopped.
2. Heavy bleeding, or shock.
3. A seizure, or a very high blood pressure in pregnancy.
4. Severe pain with a tense hernia, or cord seen or felt in labour.
5. Then pain, fever, and comfort.

## 1. How to speak in the viva

### 1. How should I enter and start?

**Answer.**  
Knock, greet the panel, and sit only when they ask you to sit. Keep your back straight, your voice slow, and your answers short. Start with "Good morning, Sir" or "Good morning, Ma'am." If you need a second to think, say, "May I take a moment?" Then answer. Thank them at the end.

**Counter questions**

**1.1 What if I do not understand the question?**  
Say, "I am sorry, could you please repeat the question?" Do not answer a different question.

**1.2 What if the examiner says my answer is wrong?**  
Stay calm. Say, "Thank you, I will remember that." Do not argue. If you are sure, you may say, "In my ward we follow this step. I am happy to learn the method you use here."

**1.3 What if they ask something I have never seen?**  
Say what you do know, then say what you would do for safety: call the senior nurse, inform the doctor, stay with the patient, and check the protocol. Do not invent a procedure.

### 2. How do I give a nursing answer, not a doctor's answer?

**Answer.**  
The examiner wants your job. After the meaning, say what the nurse does: stay with the patient, call for help, check airway, breathing, and circulation, start oxygen if needed, open a vein line if you are trained and it is allowed, record vital signs, and inform the doctor with a clear report. Do not jump to surgery or a rare drug as your first line.

**Counter questions**

**2.1 What is ABC?**  
ABC means Airway, Breathing, and Circulation. First make sure the airway is open. Then check breathing. Then check pulse, bleeding, and blood pressure.

**2.2 What is a clear report to the doctor?**  
Use SBAR. SBAR means Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation. Example: "Mrs Devi, bed 4, is bleeding heavily after a normal birth 20 minutes ago. She is a second-time mother. Her pulse is 120 and her uterus is soft. I have called for help, started oxygen, and I am massaging the uterus. Please come now. I need an order for oxytocin and blood cross-match."

## 2. Most-asked general questions

### 3. Tell me about yourself.

**Answer.** Use this shape. Fill in your own facts. Keep it under one minute.

"Good morning. My name is ______. I completed B.Sc. Nursing from ______ in ______. I have ______ years of experience, mainly in the ______ ward at ______. My daily work is admission care, medicine giving, vital signs, handover, and watching for danger signs. I have looked after emergencies such as a sudden collapse, heavy bleeding, and high-risk mothers. I speak calmly with patients and relatives. I record on time, and I inform the doctor early. I want to work as a staff nurse in your hospital and learn your protocols."

Do not read your whole resume. Do not speak badly about your old hospital.

**Counter questions**

**3.1 Which ward do you like most, and why?**  
Name a real ward. Say one skill you learned there. Example: "I like the labour room because I learned to watch the fetal heart, the uterus, and bleeding in the first hour after birth."

**3.2 What was a difficult day at work?**  
Pick a real safe example. Say the problem, what you did, and what you learned. Example: "Two patients needed me at the same time. I asked a colleague to stay with the stable patient, and I stayed with the patient who had chest pain. I informed the doctor at once. I learned to ask for help early."

### 4. Why did you choose nursing?

**Answer.**  
"I chose nursing because I want to care for people when they are sick and scared. I like practical work, not only books. A nurse stays with the patient for the whole shift. I can notice a change early and prevent harm. The work is hard, but it has a clear purpose."

**Counter questions**

**4.1 Nursing is hard. Why will you stay?**  
"The night duty and the emergencies are hard. I stay because I prepare well, I take handover properly, and I ask when I am not sure. I rest on my off days so I can work safely."

**4.2 What does a nurse do that a doctor does not do?**  
The doctor diagnoses and orders treatment. The nurse stays at the bedside, gives the care, watches the response, prevents falls and infection, teaches the patient, and reports changes early.

### 5. Why do you want to work in this hospital?

**Answer.**  
Learn two true facts before the interview: the type of hospital, and one service it is known for. Then say:

"I want to work here because this hospital has a busy ______ department and clear nursing standards. I can use my ______ years of experience, and I can learn your emergency system. I am ready for shift duty, including nights."

**Counter questions**

**5.1 Are you ready for night duty?**  
"Yes. On night duty I take a full handover, check the emergency trolley, round on serious patients first, and call the duty doctor early if a patient changes."

**5.2 What do you know about our hospital?**  
Say only what you really know: bed strength, a speciality, a teaching hospital, or a government service. Do not invent awards.

### 6. What are your strengths and one weakness?

**Answer.**  
Strengths, pick two that match the ward:

- "I stay calm in an emergency."
- "I give medicines only after checking the rights."
- "I explain care in simple words."
- "I complete records before the shift ends."

Weakness, pick one real habit that you are already improving:

"I used to feel nervous when I spoke to a senior doctor. I now use SBAR. I write the vital signs on a slip before I call. My handover is clearer."

Do not say "I have no weakness." Do not say a weakness that makes you unsafe, such as "I forget medicines."

**Counter questions**

**6.1 How do you work with a nurse you do not agree with?**  
"I listen first. If it is about patient safety, I follow the protocol and inform the in-charge. I do not argue in front of the patient."

**6.2 How do you handle a mistake?**  
"I make the patient safe first. I inform the senior nurse and the doctor at once. I do not hide it. I write what happened. I check how to prevent it next time."

### 7. Where do you see yourself in five years?

**Answer.**  
"I see myself as a confident senior staff nurse. I want to be trusted on charge duty, to guide junior nurses, and to follow updated protocols. I may later do a post-basic course in the speciality I work in, such as obstetrics or critical care, if the hospital supports it."

**Counter questions**

**7.1 Do you want to leave bedside nursing?**  
"No. I want more skill at the bedside first. Teaching juniors can come along with patient care."

### 8. What is nursing?

**Answer.**  
Nursing is the care of a person who is sick, injured, or in need of health support. The nurse promotes health, prevents illness, cares during illness, and supports recovery or a peaceful death. Virginia Henderson said the nurse helps the person do the activities that keep him or her healthy, which the person would do alone if he or she had the strength, the will, and the knowledge.

The International Council of Nurses says nursing includes care of all ages, families, and communities, and advocacy so people get equal care.

**Counter questions**

**8.1 Who is the founder of modern nursing?**  
Florence Nightingale. She linked clean air, clean water, cleanliness, light, and records with lower death rates.

**8.2 What is the difference between a profession and a job, for nursing?**  
Nursing has a body of knowledge, a licence, a code of ethics, and responsibility for the patient. It is not only task work.

**8.3 Name two roles of a nurse.**  
Caregiver, teacher, advocate, counsellor, manager, and researcher. For 1 to 3 years, the main roles are caregiver, teacher, and advocate.

### 9. What are the qualities of a good staff nurse?

**Answer.**  
A good staff nurse is punctual, honest, clean in practice, calm in emergency, kind in speech, and exact in records. She checks before she gives a medicine. She reports bad news early. She keeps secrets of the patient. She asks for help when the work is beyond her skill.

**Counter questions**

**9.1 Which quality matters most in an emergency?**  
A calm mind and a clear priority. Panic wastes time. Call for help, start basic life support, and talk in short clear sentences.

### 10. What is the nursing process?

**Answer.**  
The nursing process is the way a nurse plans care. It has five steps. A short name is ADPIE.

1. **Assessment.** Collect facts: history, vital signs, pain, wounds, and what the patient says.
2. **Diagnosis.** This is a nursing diagnosis, not a medical diagnosis. Example: "risk of bleeding" or "acute pain."
3. **Planning.** Set a goal and the steps. Example: "Uterus will stay firm, and the pad will not soak within one hour."
4. **Implementation.** Do the care.
5. **Evaluation.** Check if the goal happened. If not, change the plan.

**Counter questions**

**10.1 Give one nursing diagnosis for a mother after birth.**  
Risk of bleeding related to a soft uterus, as shown by heavy lochia and a rising pulse.

**10.2 What is the difference between a medical diagnosis and a nursing diagnosis?**  
The medical diagnosis names the disease, such as preeclampsia. The nursing diagnosis names the patient's problem that the nurse can act on, such as risk of seizure or acute pain.

**10.3 What if the evaluation shows no improvement?**  
Tell the doctor, check if the action was done correctly, and change the plan. Do not keep repeating a failed action in silence.

### 11. What are normal adult vital signs?

**Answer.**

- Temperature: about 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius. Oral is often taught as 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit.
- Pulse: 60 to 100 beats per minute. It should be regular and easy to feel.
- Respiration: 12 to 20 breaths per minute. Quiet, and not using extra muscles.
- Blood pressure: around 120/80 millimetres of mercury. A reading of 140/90 or more is high and must be reported.
- Oxygen saturation on a pulse oximeter: usually 95 to 100 percent on room air. Below 90 percent is urgent.
- Urine: at least 30 millilitres per hour, or about 0.5 millilitres per kilogram per hour.
- Pain: ask the patient. Use a score of 0 to 10.

Report a changing trend, not only one odd number. A pulse that was 80 and is now 120 matters.

**Counter questions**

**11.1 Where do you check the pulse in an emergency?**  
In an adult, the carotid pulse, on the side of the neck. In an infant, the brachial pulse, on the inside of the upper arm. Do not spend more than 10 seconds looking for a pulse.

**11.2 What is tachycardia and bradycardia?**  
Tachycardia means a fast heart rate, above 100 beats per minute in an adult. Bradycardia means a slow heart rate, below 60 beats per minute in an adult.

**11.3 How long do you count the respiration?**  
Count for a full 30 seconds and multiply by two if the breathing is regular. Count for a full minute if it is irregular, or if the patient is very ill. Do not tell the patient you are counting breaths. They may change the pattern.

**11.4 What is a normal fetal heart rate?**  
About 110 to 160 beats per minute. Below 110 or above 160 needs a prompt report, especially if it stays low after a contraction.

### 12. How do you give a medicine safely?

**Answer.**  
Check the doctor's order, the medicine card, and the label. Use the rights of giving medicine:

1. Right patient. Ask the name and check the identity band.
2. Right medicine. Check the label three times: when you take it, when you prepare it, and when you give it.
3. Right dose. Calculate, and ask a second nurse to check high-alert medicines such as insulin, heparin, and magnesium sulphate.
4. Right route. By mouth, into the muscle, into the vein, or another ordered route.
5. Right time.
6. Right documentation. Write it after you give it, not before.
7. Right reason. Know why this patient needs it.
8. Right response. Come back and see the effect.
9. Right education. Tell the patient the name and the purpose in simple words.
10. Right to refuse. If the patient refuses, do not force it. Inform the doctor and record the refusal.

Also check allergy, expiry date, and the look of the liquid. Do not give a cloudy medicine unless it is supposed to be cloudy, such as some insulin.

**Counter questions**

**12.1 What are high-alert medicines?**  
Medicines that can kill or harm badly if the dose is wrong. Examples: insulin, concentrated potassium chloride, heparin, oxytocin infusion, magnesium sulphate, chemotherapy, and sedatives.

**12.2 A patient says, "This tablet looks different." What do you do?**  
Stop. Do not convince the patient to swallow it. Check the order and the label again. Ask the pharmacy or the senior nurse. Patient doubt is a safety check.

**12.3 Can you give a verbal order?**  
Only in a real emergency, as your hospital allows. Repeat the order back, including the dose and route. Give it, and get the doctor to sign as soon as possible. Write the time.

**12.4 What is a side effect, and what is an allergy?**  
A side effect is a known extra effect, such as nausea. An allergy is an immune reaction, such as rash, swelling, wheeze, or collapse. Allergy can be an emergency.

### 13. What are the common injection sites and needle angles?

**Answer.**

- Intradermal means into the skin. Site: inner forearm. Angle: 10 to 15 degrees. A small wheal should form. Used for some skin tests.
- Subcutaneous means under the skin. Sites: abdomen, outer upper arm, outer thigh. Angle: 45 degrees, or 90 degrees if the needle is short and the fat is enough. Used for insulin and some heparin.
- Intramuscular means into the muscle. Sites: ventrogluteal (side of the hip, the safest), vastus lateralis (outer thigh), and deltoid (upper arm, small volumes only). Angle: 90 degrees.
- Intravenous means into the vein. Angle: about 15 to 30 degrees, bevel up, until you see blood flash back.

Do not use the dorsogluteal buttock site if a safer site is available. The sciatic nerve can be injured there.

**Counter questions**

**13.1 Why is the ventrogluteal site preferred?**  
It is away from major nerves and vessels. It can take a larger adult dose. The deltoid is only for small volumes, about 1 millilitre, sometimes up to 2 millilitres.

**13.2 How do you find the ventrogluteal site?**  
Place the heel of your opposite hand on the greater trochanter. Point the index finger to the anterior superior iliac spine. Spread the middle finger toward the iliac crest. Inject in the V between the index and middle fingers.

**13.3 What is the Z-track method?**  
Pull the skin and tissue to one side, inject at 90 degrees, wait about 10 seconds, withdraw the needle, then release the skin. The path seals. It keeps an irritating medicine, such as iron, from leaking into the skin. Do not massage after Z-track.

**13.4 What do you do if you see blood in the syringe before an intramuscular injection?**  
You have entered a vessel. Do not inject. Withdraw, discard the needle and syringe as per protocol, prepare a fresh dose if the medicine was not injected, and choose a new site. Follow your hospital rule on whether the drawn medicine can be reused. Many hospitals say prepare again.

### 14. What are the five moments of hand hygiene?

**Answer.**  
The World Health Organization teaches five moments:

1. Before touching a patient.
2. Before a clean or aseptic task, such as a dressing or a cannula.
3. After a body-fluid exposure risk.
4. After touching a patient.
5. After touching the patient's surroundings, such as the bed rail.

Wash with soap and water for 40 to 60 seconds when hands are visibly dirty, after the toilet, and after care of a patient with diarrhoea. Use an alcohol hand rub for 20 to 30 seconds when hands look clean.

**Counter questions**

**14.1 Name the steps of hand washing in simple words.**  
Wet hands, apply soap, rub palm to palm, rub the backs, rub between fingers, rub the backs of fingers, clean thumbs, rub fingertips on the palm, rub wrists, rinse, and dry with a clean towel. Use the towel to close the tap if it is not a sensor tap.

**14.2 When is alcohol rub not enough?**  
When hands are visibly soiled, after contact with spore-forming germs such as Clostridioides difficile diarrhoea, and before eating. Then use soap and water.

**14.3 What is aseptic technique?**  
Keeping an area free from germs during a procedure. You use sterile gloves, sterile instruments, and a no-touch method for the key parts.

### 15. How do you segregate biomedical waste in India?

**Answer.**  
Biomedical waste means waste that can spread infection. Segregate it at the point of care. Do not mix bags later. The Biomedical Waste Management Rules use these containers:

- **Yellow bag:** human body parts, placenta, blood-soaked cotton and dressings, soiled linen meant for discard, and microbiology waste. Expired medicines and some chemical waste also go in yellow, in the correct yellow container.
- **Red bag:** contaminated plastic that can be recycled, such as intravenous sets, catheters, gloves, urine bags, and syringes after the needle is removed.
- **White puncture-proof container:** sharps. Needles, blades, scalpels, and syringes with fixed needles.
- **Blue box or container:** broken glass, medicine vials, ampules, and metal implants.

General waste, such as a wrapper with no blood, goes in the general waste bin, not in these bags. Never recap a used needle with two hands.

**Counter questions**

**15.1 Where does a blood bag tubing go?**  
The plastic tubing goes in the red bag if your hospital follows the plastic-recyclable rule. A needle on it goes in the white sharps container. Follow the chart posted in your ward. If the chart differs, follow the chart.

**15.2 Where does the placenta go?**  
In the yellow bag, as anatomical waste, unless the family takes it under a proper hospital process.

**15.3 What do you do if you are stuck by a needle?**  
This is answered fully in the needle-stick question below. Short version: wash, do not suck the wound, report at once, and start post-exposure medicines if advised, as early as possible.

### 16. How do you care for an intravenous line?

**Answer.**  
An intravenous line is a small tube in a vein, used for fluids and medicines.

- Check the doctor's order for the fluid name and rate.
- Use aseptic technique to insert or to connect.
- Date the site dressing.
- Check the site every shift, and more often if the patient complains: redness, swelling, pain, or cool skin.
- Keep the line closed and the dressing dry.
- Set the rate. Do not speed a medicine that must go slowly, such as potassium or oxytocin.
- Flush and label as per protocol.
- Remove it as soon as it is no longer needed.

**Counter questions**

**16.1 What is infiltration?**  
Fluid goes into the tissue instead of the vein. The site swells, feels cool, and the drip slows. Stop the infusion, remove the cannula, raise the limb, and inform the doctor. Do not keep pushing fluid.

**16.2 What is phlebitis?**  
The vein is inflamed. The site is red, warm, painful, and may have a red line. Remove the cannula, inform the doctor, and apply care as ordered. Do not use that vein again until it settles.

**16.3 What is an air embolism?**  
Air enters the vein and can block blood flow. Prevention: prime the tubing until no air remains, and change bags before they run dry. If you suspect it, clamp the line, call for help, give oxygen, and place the patient on the left side with the head lower if your protocol says so, then follow the doctor.

**16.4 What fluids are commonly used?**  
Normal saline is 0.9 percent sodium chloride. Ringer's lactate is a balanced salt fluid, often used in bleeding and in labour. Dextrose 5 percent is sugar water. Do not mix blood with dextrose. Blood goes with normal saline.

### 17. How do you prevent infection from a urinary catheter?

**Answer.**  
A urinary catheter is a tube in the bladder. Infection from it is called CAUTI, which means Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection.

- Put it in only if it is truly needed, with a doctor's order.
- Use sterile technique for insertion.
- Keep the bag below the bladder, and off the floor.
- Do not disconnect the tube for routine samples. Use the sampling port.
- Clean the perineum daily and after stool.
- Do not clamp and unclamp without an order.
- Empty the bag with a clean jug used for that patient. Do not let the spout touch the jug.
- Remove the catheter as soon as the reason is over.
- Watch for fever, cloudy urine, and pain.

**Counter questions**

**17.1 Why is urinary retention common after hernia surgery?**  
Pain, spinal anaesthesia, and an elderly man with an enlarged prostate can all stop urine flow. Check the bladder. If the patient cannot pass urine and the bladder is full, inform the doctor. Catheterisation may be needed.

**17.2 Why does a full bladder cause bleeding after birth?**  
A full bladder pushes the uterus to the side and stops it from contracting. A soft uterus bleeds. Empty the bladder. If the mother cannot pass urine, catheterise with aseptic technique.

### 18. How do you give oxygen safely?

**Answer.**  
Oxygen is a medicine. Give it on an order, except in an emergency when the patient is blue, gasping, or saturating very low. Then start oxygen and inform the doctor at once.

- Nasal cannula: about 1 to 6 litres per minute. Comfortable for eating and talking.
- Simple face mask: about 5 to 8 litres per minute. Do not use below 5 litres, because exhaled carbon dioxide can build up in the mask.
- Face mask with a reservoir bag, sometimes called a non-rebreather: about 10 to 15 litres per minute for a very hypoxic patient. Keep the bag inflated.
- Venturi mask: gives a set oxygen percent. Useful when you must not give too much oxygen, such as some patients with COPD. COPD means Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease.

Check the saturation, the nose and ears for pressure sores from the tubing, and that the cylinder or the wall outlet is not empty. No oil or smoking near oxygen. It supports fire.

**Counter questions**

**18.1 What saturation do you aim for?**  
For most adults, 94 to 98 percent. For some patients with COPD who retain carbon dioxide, the target is often 88 to 92 percent. Follow the order. Do not remove oxygen from a blue patient to "avoid too much oxygen."

**18.2 How do you know oxygen is helping?**  
The saturation rises, the breathing slows toward normal, the colour improves, and the patient is less restless. Restlessness can be a sign of low oxygen.

### 19. What do you do if a blood transfusion reaction starts?

**Answer.**  
Stop the transfusion at once. Do not remove the cannula. Keep the vein open with a new tubing and normal saline. Call the doctor and the senior nurse. Check the patient name and the blood bag number again. Record vital signs. Send the blood bag, the tubing, and fresh blood and urine samples to the blood bank as per protocol. Stay with the patient.

Signs of a serious reaction: fever, chills, back pain or chest pain, rash, wheeze, dark urine, sudden fall in blood pressure, or collapse.

**Counter questions**

**19.1 What are the types of reaction, in simple words?**  

- Mild fever reaction: fever and chills.
- Allergic reaction: rash and itching.
- Severe allergic reaction, called anaphylaxis: wheeze, swelling, and shock.
- Haemolytic reaction: the wrong blood is being destroyed. Fever, back pain, low blood pressure, and dark urine. This is an emergency.
- Fluid overload: cough, breathlessness, and a rising pulse, more likely if the blood runs too fast in a small or heart patient.
- Germs in the bag: high fever, shock, often during or soon after the start.

**19.2 What checks do you do before starting blood?**  
Two nurses check the order, consent, name, hospital number, blood group of the patient and the bag, bag number, expiry, and leaks or clots in the bag. Record baseline temperature, pulse, and blood pressure. Start slowly. Stay for the first 15 minutes. Use the special blood set with a filter. Use normal saline. Do not add any medicine into the blood bag.

**19.3 How soon must blood be finished?**  
Usually within 4 hours of leaving controlled storage. Do not keep a blood bag on the bedside locker to "use later."

### 20. What are the types of shock?

**Answer.**  
Shock means the body tissues are not getting enough blood flow and oxygen. The patient may be cold, pale, sweaty, fast in pulse, low in blood pressure, restless, and passing little urine.

- **Hypovolemic shock:** not enough blood volume. Causes: bleeding, such as postpartum haemorrhage, dehydration, and burns.
- **Cardiogenic shock:** the heart pump is weak. Causes: a large heart attack.
- **Distributive shock:** the vessels are too wide. Types include septic shock from severe infection, anaphylactic shock from a severe allergy, and neurogenic shock from a spinal injury.
- **Obstructive shock:** a blockage. Examples: a large clot in the lung, or air or fluid pressing the heart.

Nurse's first actions are the same at the start: call for help, airway and oxygen, lay the patient flat with legs raised if not breathless, stop obvious bleeding, open a large vein line, do not give oral fluids, record vital signs and urine, and inform the doctor.

**Counter questions**

**20.1 How is septic shock different at the bedside?**  
The patient may have fever or a very low temperature, a known infection, warm flushed skin early on, and a fast pulse. Later the blood pressure stays low. Take cultures if ordered before antibiotics, but do not delay antibiotics for a long time.

**20.2 What is anaphylaxis care?**  
Stop the trigger medicine. Call for help. Oxygen. Adrenaline is the key medicine, given into the outer thigh muscle as ordered. Lay the patient flat if possible. Fluids may be ordered. Do not sit the patient up if they feel faint. Watch the airway for swelling.

**20.3 Why should you not give water by mouth in shock?**  
The patient may vomit and breathe it in, and may need surgery or anaesthesia. Give fluids through a vein.

### 21. What is the Glasgow Coma Scale?

**Answer.**  
The Glasgow Coma Scale, short form GCS, is a score of how awake a patient is. The best score is 15. The worst score is 3. You score three things:

- Eye opening: 4 points if spontaneous, 3 to speech, 2 to pain, 1 if none.
- Verbal response: 5 if oriented, 4 if confused, 3 if words do not fit, 2 if only sounds, 1 if none.
- Motor response: 6 if obeys commands, 5 if localises pain, 4 if withdraws from pain, 3 if abnormal flexion, 2 if extension, 1 if none.

A score of 13 to 15 is mild, 9 to 12 is moderate, and 8 or below is severe. A patient with GCS 8 or below cannot protect the airway. Inform the doctor at once. Prepare for airway support.

**Counter questions**

**21.1 Can you use GCS during a seizure?**  
Score after the seizure, when the patient is in the quiet phase. During the fit, protect the patient and time the fit. Do not force a score in the middle of jerking.

**21.2 What is a simple AVPU scale?**  
AVPU is quicker. A means Alert. V means responds to Voice. P means responds to Pain. U means Unresponsive. Use it in a rush, then do a full GCS when the patient is safer.

### 22. What is low blood sugar, and what do you do?

**Answer.**  
Low blood sugar is hypoglycaemia. A common alert level is below 70 milligrams per decilitre. Signs: sweating, trembling, hunger, fast pulse, headache, confusion, strange behaviour, seizure, or unconsciousness. It can look like drunkenness.

If the patient is conscious and can swallow: give about 15 grams of fast sugar, such as glucose powder in water, or a glucose drink as available. Recheck in 15 minutes. Repeat if still low. Then give a small snack if a meal is not due. Inform the doctor.

If the patient is unconscious or cannot swallow: do not put anything in the mouth. Call for help. Place on the side. Check the sugar. Give intravenous dextrose as ordered, often 25 percent or 50 percent dextrose. If there is no vein line, glucagon into the muscle may be ordered. Recheck the sugar.

**Counter questions**

**22.1 What is high blood sugar at the bedside?**  
Hyperglycaemia. The patient is thirsty, passes a lot of urine, and may be dry and drowsy. In diabetic ketoacidosis there is deep fast breathing, a fruity smell, abdominal pain, and vomiting. Check sugar, inform the doctor, do not give sugar water, and follow the insulin and fluid order. This patient can also have low potassium. Do not treat it as a simple high number.

**22.2 Why is low sugar more urgent than a moderately high sugar?**  
The brain needs sugar every minute. Low sugar can cause brain injury and death quickly. A moderately high sugar is serious, but a conscious patient with low sugar must be treated at once.

### 23. What are pressure sores, and how do you prevent them?

**Answer.**  
A pressure sore, also called a pressure injury or bedsore, is skin and tissue damage from pressure, usually over a bony point such as the sacrum, heel, or hip.

Stages in simple words:

1. Stage 1: skin is intact, but a red area does not turn white when you press it.
2. Stage 2: partial skin loss, a blister, or a shallow open area.
3. Stage 3: full skin loss. Fat may be seen. Bone is not seen.
4. Stage 4: full loss with bone, tendon, or muscle seen.
5. Unstageable: the base is covered by dead black or yellow tissue, so you cannot see the depth.
6. Deep tissue injury: skin may be intact but purple or maroon, or a blood blister.

Prevention: turn every 2 hours, keep skin clean and dry, manage urine and stool, use a pressure-relieving mattress if available, lift the heels off the bed, keep the sheet free of wrinkles, encourage protein in the diet if allowed, and do not rub a red area hard.

**Counter questions**

**23.1 What is the Braden scale?**  
A score of pressure-sore risk. It looks at feeling, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction. A low score means high risk. You then turn the patient more carefully and inspect the skin every shift.

**23.2 Can you massage a red pressure area?**  
No. Massage can damage the tissue under the skin. Remove the pressure instead.

### 24. What do you do after a needle-stick injury?

**Answer.**  
A needle-stick is when a used needle pierces your skin. Blood-borne viruses of concern are HIV, HBV, and HCV.

- HIV means Human Immunodeficiency Virus.
- HBV means Hepatitis B Virus.
- HCV means Hepatitis C Virus.

Steps:

1. Do not panic, and do not suck the wound.
2. Wash with soap and running water. Do not scrub hard. Do not apply bleach to the skin.
3. Let it bleed gently. Do not squeeze violently.
4. Inform the in-charge and the infection-control nurse at once.
5. Fill the incident form. Note the patient's identity, the time, and the type of needle.
6. The source patient's blood is tested as per hospital consent rules.
7. Your blood is tested for baseline HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C.
8. Post-exposure prophylaxis, short form PEP, for HIV is started as early as possible if the risk is significant. Best within 2 hours. It can still be started up to 72 hours. It is given for 28 days. The infection-control doctor chooses the tablets. A common Indian regimen uses tenofovir, lamivudine, and dolutegravir.
9. For hepatitis B: if you are vaccinated and your antibody level is enough, you may need no extra hepatitis B treatment. If you are not protected, hepatitis B immunoglobulin and the vaccine may be given. There is no reliable PEP for hepatitis C. You need follow-up tests.
10. Use condoms and follow the counselling advice until follow-up tests are clear. Do not donate blood during follow-up.

**Counter questions**

**24.1 Which needle-stick is higher risk?**  
A hollow needle with visible blood, a deep injury, a needle that was in an artery or vein, and a source patient with a high viral load. A solid suture needle is lower risk, but you still report it.

**24.2 How do you prevent needle-stick injuries?**  
Do not recap with two hands. Use a sharps container close to the point of use. Do not fill the container above the marked line. Do not pass an open needle from hand to hand. Wear gloves. Discard the needle yourself. Never put a needle in a yellow or red bag.

### 25. What is informed consent?

**Answer.**  
Informed consent means the patient agrees to a procedure after understanding it. The explanation includes the nature of the procedure, the benefit, the important risks, and the alternatives. The patient must be an adult with the capacity to decide, and the agreement must be voluntary. The doctor who does the procedure explains the surgery. The nurse checks that the form is signed, that the patient had a chance to ask questions, and that the right patient is going for the right procedure. The nurse does not force a signature.

**Counter questions**

**25.1 Can a relative sign?**  
If the patient is unconscious, a minor, or not able to decide, the legal guardian or the person allowed by hospital policy signs. In a life-saving emergency, treatment may proceed under hospital policy while the team documents why consent could not wait. Follow your hospital rule.

**25.2 A patient signed, then says "I do not want it." What do you do?**  
Stop. Do not take the patient to the operation theatre. Inform the doctor. A patient can withdraw consent. Record the refusal.

**25.3 What is implied consent?**  
For simple safe care, such as checking the pulse, the patient's cooperation can imply consent. You still explain. Implied consent is not enough for surgery or blood transfusion.

### 26. Why is documentation important?

**Answer.**  
The record is a legal document and a communication tool. If it is not written, it is treated as not done. Write the time, the fact, the action, and the response. Write in clear words. Do not erase with a scribble that hides the old words. Put a single line through a mistake, write "error," sign, and write the correct note. Do not chart a medicine before you give it. Do not chart for another nurse.

**Counter questions**

**26.1 What do you write after an emergency?**  
Time the event started, the patient's condition, vital signs, what you did, which doctor you called and at what time, the orders, the medicines, the response, and the names of staff who helped. Write as soon as the patient is safe. Do not rely on memory at the end of the shift.

**26.2 Can you use abbreviations in the chart?**  
Use only the abbreviations your hospital allows. Do not invent short forms. "U" for units and "MS" for medicines have caused deaths. Write "units" in full.

### 27. What is confidentiality?

**Answer.**  
Confidentiality means you do not share a patient's illness, test results, or personal story with people who are not in the care team. You do not discuss a patient in the lift, canteen, or on social media. You do not take photos of patients. You can share information with the treating team, and when the law requires it, such as some police or court situations, through the proper hospital channel.

**Counter questions**

**27.1 A relative asks for the diagnosis in the corridor. What do you say?**  
"I want to keep this private. Please come with me, and we will speak with the doctor where others cannot hear." Do not give a new diagnosis in a public corridor. Check what the patient allows you to tell.

**27.2 Your friend is admitted. Can you open the file out of concern?**  
No. Open a file only if you are part of the care, or if you are officially asked. Curiosity is not a reason.

### 28. What is your role when a code blue is called?

**Answer.**  
Code blue means a patient has had a cardiac or respiratory arrest, or is about to have one. Your role:

1. Note the time. Shout for help and activate the code as per your hospital, often a bell and a phone number.
2. Start chest compressions if there is no normal breathing and no pulse. Send someone for the defibrillator and the crash cart.
3. Bring the crash cart to the bed. Clear space. Put the bed flat. Remove the headboard if needed.
4. Attach oxygen and the monitor or the defibrillator pads when they arrive.
5. Give medicines only from the doctor's or the team leader's order, and say the dose back.
6. One nurse records times, rhythms, shocks, and drugs.
7. Crowd control: ask extra people to step out.
8. After the event, restock the cart, send lab samples, talk to the family through the doctor, and write the notes.

Follow the team leader. Do not give random medicines.

**Counter questions**

**28.1 What is in a crash cart?**  
A defibrillator, oxygen and a bag-mask, airway tubes, suction, intravenous cannulas, fluids, emergency medicines, a sharp box, and a checklist. The cart is locked or sealed. Check the seal and the expiry every shift. Common medicines examiners ask: adrenaline, amiodarone, atropine, adenosine, dextrose, calcium gluconate, normal saline, and naloxone. Exact layout follows your hospital.

**28.2 What is the difference between defibrillation and cardioversion?**  
Defibrillation is an unsynchronised shock for a deadly rhythm with no pulse, such as ventricular fibrillation. Cardioversion is a synchronised shock for some fast rhythms when the patient still has a pulse. It is timed with the heart's electrical wave so it does not strike at a dangerous moment. The nurse does not shock a patient who has a pulse unless a trained doctor orders and supervises it.

### 29. What is triage?

**Answer.**  
Triage means sorting patients by who needs care first, not by who came first. A common colour system:

- **Red:** immediate. Life is in danger now. Example: not breathing, heavy bleeding, shock.
- **Yellow:** urgent. Serious, but can wait a short time.
- **Green:** minor. The person can walk and wait.
- **Black:** dead, or injuries so severe that survival is not expected in a mass disaster. In a normal ward you still call the doctor. You do not decide "black" alone.

**Counter questions**

**29.1 In your ward, two bells ring. Who do you see first?**  
See the patient with an airway problem, a seizure, heavy bleeding, or chest pain before the patient who wants a water refill or a discharge paper. Tell the second patient you will return. Ask a colleague to check the second room if you can.

## 3. CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation)

CPR means Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation. Cardio means the heart. Pulmonary means the lungs. Resuscitation means bringing back life support. You press the chest and give breaths so blood and oxygen keep moving when the heart has stopped, or when breathing is not normal.

These steps follow the basic life support method taught in Indian hospitals, based on the American Heart Association basic life support approach. Your hospital code policy is the final word on the ward.

### 30. When do you start CPR?

**Answer.**  
Start CPR when an adult is unresponsive and is not breathing normally, and you do not feel a pulse within 10 seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing. Gasping is a sign to start CPR. Do not wait for a doctor to arrive before you start compressions.

**Counter questions**

**30.1 What do you do in the first 10 seconds?**  
Make the scene safe. Tap the shoulders and shout, "Are you all right?" If there is no response, shout for help. Send one person to call the code and to bring the AED. AED means Automated External Defibrillator. Check breathing and the carotid pulse together for no more than 10 seconds. If there is no normal breathing and no definite pulse, start compressions.

**30.2 What if I am not sure about the pulse?**  
Start compressions. A short delay is more harmful than starting CPR on a person who might have had a very weak pulse. In the hospital, the team will attach the monitor quickly.

**30.3 What is the chain of survival?**  
Early recognition and call for help, early CPR, early defibrillation, and early advanced care, then care after the heart restarts. As a nurse, you own the first three until the code team arrives.

### 31. How do you give adult chest compressions?

**Answer.**

- Put the person on a firm flat surface. If they are on a soft mattress, use a CPR board if you have one, or press correctly and accept that the board is coming.
- Kneel or stand beside the chest.
- Place the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, in the centre of the chest. Put the other hand on top. Lock your fingers.
- Keep your arms straight. Your shoulders are above your hands.
- Press hard and fast. Depth is about 5 to 6 centimetres. Do not press deeper than 6 centimetres.
- Rate is 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
- Let the chest come fully back up after each press. This is full recoil. Do not lean on the chest.
- Do not interrupt compressions for more than 10 seconds.
- Ratio when there is no advanced airway: 30 compressions, then 2 breaths.
- Change the person who is compressing about every 2 minutes, so quality stays high. Change quickly.

**Counter questions**

**31.1 Why is full recoil important?**  
The heart fills with blood when the chest comes up. If you lean on the chest, the heart does not fill, and the next compression pushes less blood.

**31.2 Why 30 to 2?**  
This ratio gives enough presses to move blood, and enough breaths to add oxygen, without long stops. Too many breaths, or breaths given too hard, reduce blood flow.

**31.3 Where exactly is the hand position?**  
Lower half of the sternum, centre of the chest. Not on the upper abdomen, and not on the ribs at the side. Wrong position breaks ribs in the wrong place and gives poor flow. Some rib injury can still happen with correct CPR. Correct CPR is still the right choice when the heart has stopped.

**31.4 What is high-quality CPR?**  
Right depth, right rate, full recoil, minimal pauses, and breaths that make the chest rise. Also switch compressor every 2 minutes.

### 32. How do you open the airway and give breaths?

**Answer.**  
Use the head-tilt and chin-lift if there is no suspected neck injury. If a neck injury is likely, use the jaw thrust if you are trained. Pinch the nose. Give two breaths with a bag-mask device, each over about 1 second, just enough to see the chest rise. Avoid a huge breath. If the chest does not rise, reopen the airway and try again. Then go back to compressions.

In the hospital, use a bag-mask with oxygen as soon as it arrives. A pocket mask with a one-way valve is better than a mouth-to-mouth breath. Mouth-to-mouth is for when no device is available.

**Counter questions**

**32.1 What if there is an advanced airway, such as an endotracheal tube?**  
Do not stop compressions for breaths. Compress continuously at 100 to 120 per minute. Give one breath every 6 seconds, which is about 10 breaths per minute. The person on the airway must not over-ventilate.

**32.2 What is the sniffing position?**  
The head is in a position as if the person is sniffing a flower. The neck is slightly flexed and the head is slightly extended. It lines up the airway. Do not hyperextend an infant's neck. For an infant, keep the head in a neutral position.

**32.3 What is gastric inflation?**  
Air goes into the stomach because breaths are too hard or the airway is not open. The stomach swells, the diaphragm rises, and the person may vomit. Prevention: open the airway, give breaths slowly, and only until the chest rises.

### 33. How do you use an AED?

**Answer.**  
An AED is a machine that reads the heart rhythm and tells you if a shock is needed.

1. Switch it on. Follow the voice prompts.
2. Bare the chest and wipe it dry.
3. Attach the pads. One pad goes on the upper right chest, below the collar bone. The other goes on the lower left side of the chest, a few centimetres below the armpit. Follow the pictures on the pads.
4. Do not touch the patient while the AED analyses.
5. If it says shock, shout "Clear," look to see that nobody is touching the patient or the bed, and press the shock button.
6. Start compressions again at once. Do not stop to check the pulse at that moment unless the patient clearly wakes and breathes normally.
7. The AED will recheck about every 2 minutes.

**Counter questions**

**33.1 What if the chest is hairy and the pad will not stick?**  
Use the razor in the AED kit if there is one, or press firmly. Some kits include a spare set of pads to pull hair off. Do not delay for a long shave.

**33.2 What if the patient is wet?**  
Move off a pool of water if you can do it quickly. Wipe the chest dry. Then attach pads. Water on the skin can divert the shock.

**33.3 Can you use an AED on a patient with a medicine patch on the chest?**  
Remove the patch with a gloved hand and wipe the skin. Do not place a pad over a patch. A shock over a patch can burn.

**33.4 What is a shockable rhythm?**  
Ventricular fibrillation, short form VF, which is a quivering heart with no pulse. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia, short form VT, which is a very fast rhythm from the lower chambers with no pulse. The AED decides. You do not need to read the rhythm to use an AED.

**33.5 What is a non-shockable rhythm?**  
Asystole, which is a flat line, and PEA. PEA means Pulseless Electrical Activity. The monitor may show a rhythm, but there is no pulse. The AED will say no shock. Continue high-quality CPR and find the cause. Adrenaline is used by the code team. Do not stop CPR just because the AED says no shock.

### 34. How is child and infant CPR different?

**Answer.**  
A child, for basic life support, is about 1 year to puberty. An infant is under 1 year.

- If you are alone with a child or infant and the collapse was sudden, and you saw it: call for help and get the AED, then start CPR. In a hospital, the code is called at once anyway.
- If you did not see the collapse, and you are alone outside the hospital: start CPR first for about 2 minutes, then call, because the cause is often a breathing problem. In the hospital, send someone to call while you start.
- Compression to breath ratio: one rescuer, 30 to 2. Two rescuers for a child or infant, 15 to 2.
- Child depth: about one third of the chest, about 5 centimetres. Use one or two hands.
- Infant depth: about one third of the chest, about 4 centimetres. Use the two-thumb method, with your hands encircling the chest. If your hands cannot go around the chest, use the heel of one hand. This is the current teaching. The older two-finger method is no longer the first method to describe.
- Pulse: brachial artery in an infant, carotid or femoral in a child.
- AED: use paediatric pads for an infant or a small child if available. If only adult pads are available, use them. Do not let the pads touch each other. One can be on the front and one on the back if the chest is small.

**Counter questions**

**34.1 Why is the ratio 15 to 2 with two rescuers in a child?**  
Children more often stop because of a breathing problem. More frequent breaths are used when two people can do it without long pauses.

**34.2 What is the most common cause of cardiac arrest in a child?**  
A breathing problem or low oxygen, such as choking, drowning, or severe illness. That is why breaths matter. In an adult, the most common cause of a sudden collapse is a heart rhythm problem, so early defibrillation matters.

### 35. What do you do for choking?

**Answer.**  
Choking means a foreign body is blocking the airway.

The current basic life support method, from the 2025 American Heart Association guidance, is sets of 5 back blows and then 5 thrusts. Some older teachers still say abdominal thrusts only. Say the current method first.

**Adult or child who is conscious and cannot speak, cough, or breathe:**  
Ask, "Are you choking?" Stand to the side and slightly behind. Support the chest with one hand. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand. If the object does not come out, stand behind the person and give 5 abdominal thrusts. This thrust is the Heimlich manoeuvre. Make a fist above the navel and below the breastbone. Grasp it with the other hand. Pull inward and upward sharply. Repeat cycles of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or the person becomes unconscious.

**Pregnant woman or a very obese adult:**  
Do not use abdominal thrusts. Give 5 back blows, then 5 chest thrusts on the centre of the breastbone. Repeat those cycles.

**Infant who is conscious but cannot cry or cough:**  
Do not use abdominal thrusts. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the infant face down on your forearm, head lower than the chest. Then turn the infant and give 5 chest thrusts on the lower half of the breastbone, about one third of the chest depth. Repeat 5 and 5.

**If the person becomes unconscious:**  
Lower them safely. Call the code. Start CPR. Before each breath, look in the mouth. Remove an object only if you can see it. Do not do a blind finger sweep. A blind sweep can push the object deeper.

**Counter questions**

**35.1 A person is coughing loudly. Do you start back blows?**  
No. A loud cough means the airway is partly open. Encourage coughing. Stay with them. Start back blows and thrusts only when they cannot cough, speak, or breathe.

**35.2 What is a blind finger sweep?**  
Putting a finger into the mouth when you cannot see the object. Do not do it. You may push the object down.

### 36. What is the recovery position, and when do you use it?

**Answer.**  
Use the recovery position when the person is unconscious but is breathing normally and has a pulse, and there is no suspected spinal injury. It keeps the tongue and vomit from blocking the airway.

Turn the person onto the side. The lower arm supports the head, or the upper hand supports the cheek. Bend the upper knee so the body does not roll onto the face. Tilt the head back slightly so the mouth points down. Keep checking breathing. Do not leave them alone.

**Counter questions**

**36.1 What if you suspect a neck injury and they are breathing?**  
Do not twist the neck. Hold the head in line with the body. Use a jaw thrust if the airway needs opening. Call for help. If they must be turned because of vomiting, use a log roll with helpers so the spine stays straight.

### 37. When do you stop CPR?

**Answer.**  
Stop only if one of these is true:

- The person starts breathing normally and has a pulse. This is ROSC. ROSC means Return Of Spontaneous Circulation.
- A doctor, following hospital policy, tells the team to stop.
- The scene becomes unsafe.
- There is a valid Do Not Resuscitate order. Do Not Resuscitate is often shortened to DNR. You must see the valid order. A relative's verbal wish, without a valid order, is not enough to stop in most Indian hospital emergencies. Call the doctor.
- You are completely exhausted and there is nobody to take over. In a hospital this should not happen, because you call for help.

Do not stop just because you are tired, or because the AED says "no shock."

**Counter questions**

**37.1 What are the signs of ROSC?**  
A definite pulse returns, the person may start breathing or coughing, the colour may improve, and the monitor shows an organised rhythm with a pulse. Check the pulse for no more than 10 seconds. If you are not sure, resume compressions.

**37.2 What care do you give after ROSC?**  
Keep the airway supported, give oxygen to the ordered saturation, check blood pressure, do a blood sugar, do not give too many breaths, keep the patient warm, and prepare for transfer to a critical care bed. Inform the senior doctor. This is called post-cardiac arrest care.

### 38. How is CPR different in a pregnant woman?

**Answer.**  
Call for help early, and call the obstetric team and the newborn team as well as the code team. Start the same high-quality chest compressions, 30 to 2, on the lower half of the breastbone. Manually push the uterus to the left with one hand, so the heavy uterus does not press the big vein. If enough people are present, one person does only this leftward push. Do not tilt the whole body in a way that makes compressions weak. Prepare for a possible emergency caesarean birth if the heart does not restart quickly. The target often taught is to deliver the baby by about 5 minutes if there is no ROSC, because this can help the mother and the baby. The obstetrician decides and performs it. The nurse calls the team, prepares the trolley, and continues CPR until told to stop.

**Counter questions**

**38.1 Why push the uterus to the left?**  
After about 20 weeks, the uterus presses the inferior vena cava when the mother lies flat. Less blood returns to the heart. Chest compressions then move very little blood. Leftward displacement of the uterus relieves that pressure.

**38.2 Is the hand position different?**  
Use the same lower half of the sternum. Do not move the hands up onto the upper chest. The uterus is pushed by a second person, not by moving the compression point onto the abdomen.

### 39. What medicines are used in a hospital cardiac arrest?

**Answer.**  
These are given by the code team through a vein or an intraosseous line. Intraosseous means into the bone marrow, used when a vein cannot be found. Say the name and dose back before you give it. Interview answers:

- **Adrenaline (also called epinephrine):** 1 milligram intravenously every 3 to 5 minutes during arrest. It is usually the 1 in 10,000 strength for intravenous arrest doses. It constricts vessels so more blood goes to the heart and brain during compressions.
- **Amiodarone:** for a shockable rhythm that continues after defibrillation. A common first dose is 300 milligrams intravenous, then 150 milligrams if needed.
- **Lidocaine:** an alternative if amiodarone is not available.
- **Atropine:** not a routine medicine for asystole or PEA. It is used for a slow pulse when the patient still has a pulse, commonly 1 milligram intravenously, repeated, up to 3 milligrams.
- **For low blood sugar found during the arrest:** intravenous dextrose.

Do not give these from memory on your own during a code. The leader orders them. Flush the line after each medicine so it reaches the heart. Raise the arm if the line is peripheral.

**Counter questions**

**39.1 What are the reversible causes, the Hs and Ts?**  
The team looks for causes that can be fixed:

- Hypovolemia: low blood volume, including bleeding.
- Hypoxia: low oxygen.
- Hydrogen ion, meaning acidosis.
- Hypo or hyperkalaemia: low or high potassium.
- Hypothermia: very low body temperature.
- Tension pneumothorax: air collapsing the lung and pushing the heart.
- Tamponade: fluid around the heart.
- Thrombosis of the coronary artery: a heart attack.
- Thrombosis of the lung: a massive clot in the lung.
- Toxins: poisoning or a drug overdose.

As a nurse, you can help by knowing the history: dialysis patient, trauma, asthma, recent birth with bleeding, or a missed potassium infusion.

**39.2 What do you record during the code?**  
Start time, time of each rhythm check, shocks and their energy in joules, medicines with dose and time, airway time, pulse checks, ROSC time, and who led the code. A joule is the unit of shock strength shown on the defibrillator.

### 40. What is the difference between basic life support and advanced life support?

**Answer.**  
Basic life support, often shortened to BLS, is what you start at once. It is recognition, calling for help, chest compressions, breaths, choking care, the recovery position, and the automated external defibrillator.

Advanced cardiovascular life support, often shortened to ACLS, is what the code team adds. It includes reading the heart rhythm, giving emergency medicines, placing an advanced airway, and leading the team. A ward nurse does not wait for ACLS before starting compressions. You start basic life support, then assist the advanced team.

**Counter questions**

**40.1 The bed is soft and high. Does that matter?**  
Yes. Press on a firm surface. If a CPR board is available, slide it under the chest quickly. The 2025 guidance also says the patient's chest should be about at the level of your knees, so you can press straight down without standing on tiptoe or bending too low. Lower or raise the bed.

**40.2 Is CPR different if the patient is obese?**  
No. Use the same place, rate, depth, and ratio. You may need to press firmly to reach 5 centimetres. Do not delay.

**40.3 What injuries can CPR cause?**  
Rib or breastbone fracture, vomiting, air in the stomach, and, if the hands are too low, injury to the liver. These can happen even with correct CPR. They are not a reason to stop when there is no pulse.

### 41. The patient has a pulse but is not breathing. What do you do?

**Answer.**  
This is respiratory arrest, not cardiac arrest. Do not start chest compressions while a definite pulse is present. Call for help. Open the airway. Give 1 breath every 6 seconds, which is about 10 breaths per minute, with a bag-mask and oxygen. Each breath should just make the chest rise. Recheck the pulse every 2 minutes. If the pulse disappears, start full CPR at 30 compressions to 2 breaths.

**Counter questions**

**41.1 How is this different from gasping during cardiac arrest?**  
Gasping is not normal breathing, and there is no pulse. That person needs compressions. A person with a clear pulse who is not breathing needs breaths, not compressions.

**41.2 What if you are not sure the pulse is real?**  
Start compressions. A doubtful pulse is treated as no pulse.

### 42. What do you do in a suspected opioid overdose?

**Answer.**  
An opioid is a strong pain or sedative drug, such as morphine, pethidine, or heroin. The person may be very sleepy, breathing very slowly, and have pinpoint pupils.

1. Call for help.
2. Open the airway and support breathing. Breaths come before the antidote.
3. If there is no pulse, start CPR.
4. Naloxone is the antidote. Give it if your protocol allows, or when the doctor orders it. It can be given into the muscle or into the vein. It wears off, so the person can stop breathing again. Stay and watch.
5. Naloxone does not replace CPR or breaths.

**Counter questions**

**42.1 The person wakes after naloxone and wants to leave. What do you do?**  
Do not leave them alone. The opioid can outlast the naloxone. Ask them to stay, inform the doctor, and keep checking breathing.

### 43. What is the nurse's role when a patient collapses on the ward?

**Answer.**  
This is the practical answer examiners like.

1. Make sure the bed area is safe. Lower the bed. Remove pillows from under the head for compressions.
2. Check response, breathing, and pulse for no more than 10 seconds.
3. Shout for help. Ask one named person, "You, call code blue and bring the crash cart." Point at the person.
4. Start compressions if there is no pulse and no normal breathing.
5. Ask another person to bring oxygen and the bag-mask.
6. When the AED or defibrillator arrives, attach pads while compressions continue with the shortest pause.
7. Give history to the team leader in one or two lines: diagnosis, last medicines, and what you saw.
8. After the event, inform the relative through the proper person, write the notes, and replace used items.

**Counter questions**

**43.1 The relative is crying and holding the patient. What do you do?**  
Use a calm short sentence: "I need to press the chest now to help the heart. Please stand just here, where you can see that we are helping." If they block care, a colleague gently moves them a step back. Do not shout at them.

**43.2 Should you start oxygen before compressions?**  
No. If there is no pulse, compressions come first. Oxygen and the bag are added as fast as possible, but you do not delay the first compressions to look for a mask.

## 4. Hernia

### 44. What is a hernia?

**Answer.**  
A hernia is the bulging of an organ, or part of an organ, through a weak spot in the wall that normally holds it. In the hernia examiners usually mean, a loop of bowel or omentum pushes through a weak spot in the abdominal wall. Omentum is the fatty apron inside the abdomen.

A hernia has parts:

- **Sac:** the lining that forms the pouch. In groin hernias it is often peritoneum, which is the thin sheet that lines the inside of the abdomen.
- **Coverings:** the layers of the abdominal wall over the sac.
- **Contents:** what is inside, such as bowel or omentum.
- **Neck:** the narrow opening. The neck is the dangerous part. If it is tight, it can cut off the blood supply.

**Counter questions**

**44.1 Why does the neck matter?**  
A wide neck may let the bowel go in and out. A tight neck can trap the bowel. Trapped bowel can swell, block, and then lose its blood supply. That is strangulation. It is an emergency.

**44.2 What is the difference between a hernia and a hydrocele?**  
A hydrocele is fluid around the testis. It shines when you pass a light through it in a dark room. This is transillumination. A hernia usually does not shine, because it contains bowel. A hernia may have a cough impulse. A hydrocele does not. A hernia can extend from the groin. You still inform the doctor. Do not diagnose alone if you are unsure.

### 45. What are the common types of hernia?

**Answer.**  
By place:

- **Inguinal hernia:** in the groin. The most common. More in men.
- **Femoral hernia:** just below the groin crease. More in women. Higher chance of strangulation.
- **Umbilical hernia:** at the navel. Common in infants. Often closes. In adults it needs attention if it persists.
- **Para-umbilical hernia:** just beside the navel, in adults, often in women who have had several pregnancies.
- **Incisional hernia:** through an old operation scar.
- **Epigastric hernia:** in the midline, between the navel and the chest.
- **Hiatus hernia:** part of the stomach slides up through the diaphragm into the chest. This one is inside. It causes heartburn more than a groin lump.

By behaviour:

- **Reducible:** you can push it back, or it goes back when the person lies down.
- **Irreducible, also called incarcerated:** it will not go back, but the blood supply is still present.
- **Obstructed:** the bowel is blocked. Pain, vomiting, no flatus, and a tense hernia.
- **Strangulated:** the blood supply is cut off. This is a surgical emergency.
- **Inflamed:** the contents are inflamed, for example an appendix inside the sac.

**Counter questions**

**45.1 Which hernia strangulates most easily?**  
Femoral hernia, because the femoral canal is narrow and rigid.

**45.2 Why is inguinal hernia more common in men?**  
The testis descends from the abdomen into the scrotum before birth. It leaves a path. If that path does not close, a hernia can form. Men also do heavy straining work more often, but the anatomical path is the main reason taught in viva.

**45.3 Why is femoral hernia more common in women?**  
The female pelvis is wider, and the femoral canal is relatively wider. Pregnancy and straining also raise pressure in the abdomen.

**45.4 How do you tell an inguinal hernia from a femoral hernia at the bedside?**  
Find the pubic tubercle, the bony point at the side of the pubic bone. An inguinal hernia is above and medial to the pubic tubercle. A femoral hernia is below and lateral to the pubic tubercle. If you are unsure, do not push hard. Inform the doctor.

### 46. What is the difference between an indirect and a direct inguinal hernia?

**Answer.**  
This is one of the most asked surgical questions.

**Indirect inguinal hernia**

- The bowel comes through the deep inguinal ring.
- It is lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels.
- It can pass down the inguinal canal into the scrotum.
- It is more common in infants, children, and young adults.
- It is often due to a passage that stayed open from birth, called a patent processus vaginalis.
- It can become very large.

**Direct inguinal hernia**

- The bowel pushes straight through a weak area in the back wall of the inguinal canal.
- That weak area is Hesselbach's triangle.
- It is medial to the inferior epigastric vessels.
- It rarely goes into the scrotum.
- It is more common in older men.
- It is due to weak muscles, chronic cough, straining, and heavy lifting.
- Strangulation is less common than in an indirect hernia, but it can still happen.

**Hesselbach's triangle** is bounded by:

- Lateral side: the inferior epigastric artery.
- Medial side: the outer border of the rectus abdominis muscle.
- Below: the inguinal ligament.

**Counter questions**

**46.1 What is the deep ring occlusion test?**  
The doctor reduces the hernia, then blocks the deep ring with a thumb and asks the patient to cough. If the swelling still comes out, it is likely direct, because a direct hernia does not come through the deep ring. If it does not come out while the deep ring is blocked, it is likely indirect. This is a doctor's examination. Do not do it on a painful tense hernia.

**46.2 What is a pantaloon hernia?**  
Both a direct and an indirect inguinal hernia are present on the same side. The inferior epigastric vessels lie between them, like the crotch of a pair of trousers.

**46.3 What is a Richter's hernia?**  
Only part of the wall of the bowel is trapped, not the whole width. The person can strangulate that part and still pass some stool. So the absence of full obstruction does not rule out danger. Increasing pain and a tender lump still need an urgent doctor review.

**46.4 What is a sliding hernia?**  
Part of the sac wall is made by a bowel organ itself, such as the caecum or the bladder. Surgery needs extra care so that organ is not injured. This is the surgeon's point. The nurse's point is careful urine output and abdominal checks after surgery.

### 47. What causes a hernia, and how do you prevent it?

**Answer.**  
Anything that weakens the wall, or anything that raises pressure inside the abdomen:

- Chronic cough, including smoking and tuberculosis cough.
- Chronic constipation and straining.
- Difficulty passing urine from an enlarged prostate.
- Heavy lifting.
- Obesity.
- Pregnancy and many births.
- Old age and weak muscles.
- A previous wound infection, which can lead to an incisional hernia.
- Prematurity in infant umbilical and inguinal hernia.

Prevention teaching: treat the cough, give a high-fibre diet and fluids if allowed, treat prostate symptoms, lose weight if obese, use a proper lifting method, and stop smoking. A belt or truss is not a cure. It is sometimes a temporary support only if a doctor advises it. It is dangerous if the hernia is irreducible, because it may press on trapped bowel.

**Counter questions**

**47.1 Why do you treat cough and constipation before a planned hernia operation?**  
If the patient coughs or strains hard after repair, the repair can tear and the hernia can come back. The nurse reports a new cold, a smoker's cough, or no bowel movement before the operation.

### 48. What are the signs of a strangulated hernia?

**Answer.**  
Strangulation means the blood supply of the contents is cut off. It can die, perforate, and cause peritonitis and shock. Signs:

- Sudden severe pain in the hernia, which was earlier painless or only mildly painful.
- The lump is tense, tender, and will not go back.
- There is no cough impulse.
- Nausea and vomiting.
- The abdomen may swell.
- No flatus and no stool if the bowel is obstructed.
- Fast pulse, fever later, and low blood pressure if gangrene or perforation has started.
- The skin over the lump may become red.

This is an emergency. The patient needs the operating theatre, not a clinic visit tomorrow.

**Counter questions**

**48.1 What do you do as the nurse when you suspect strangulation?**  

1. Do not try to force the lump back. Forced reduction can push dead bowel into the abdomen, or rupture it.
2. Keep the patient nil by mouth. Nil by mouth is often shortened to NPO, from the Latin nil per os, meaning nothing by mouth.
3. Inform the surgeon at once.
4. Start oxygen if the patient is in shock.
5. Open a vein line and start fluids only as ordered.
6. A nasogastric tube may be ordered to empty the stomach. Nasogastric means from the nose to the stomach.
7. Send blood for haemoglobin, group and cross-match, urea, and electrolytes as ordered.
8. Give pain relief only as ordered, after the surgeon has seen the patient. Do not mask the signs before the examination if the doctor is about to arrive in a few minutes. Do not leave the patient in severe pain for a long time either. Tell the doctor the pain score.
9. Prepare for emergency surgery: consent, theatre clothes, remove dentures, mark the site as per policy, and check the identity band.
10. Record pulse, blood pressure, pain, vomiting, and abdominal girth.

**48.2 What is the difference between obstructed and strangulated?**  
In obstruction, the lumen is blocked. The patient has colicky pain, vomiting, and constipation. The blood supply may still be present. In strangulation, the blood supply is cut off. The pain becomes constant and severe, the lump is very tender, and the patient becomes toxic. Strangulation can follow obstruction. Both need the surgeon. Strangulation cannot wait.

**48.3 A hernia suddenly goes back by itself after hours of pain. Is the patient safe?**  
Not always. Dead bowel may have slipped back inside. Watch for rising pulse, fever, abdominal pain, and guarding. Inform the surgeon. The patient may still need observation or surgery.

### 49. What are the operations for hernia?

**Answer.**

- **Herniotomy:** removal of the sac. This is the usual operation in children. A mesh is not usually used in small children.
- **Herniorrhaphy:** repair of the back wall with stitches, without a mesh. Older methods include Bassini and Shouldice repairs.
- **Hernioplasty:** repair using a mesh, so the repair is tension-free. The common open method is the Lichtenstein mesh repair. This is the usual adult operation now. It can also be done by laparoscopy. Laparoscopy means surgery through small cuts with a camera.

The nurse does not choose the operation. You should know the names, because the examiner asks, and because mesh versus suture changes the discharge advice slightly. Mesh infection is a serious complication.

**Counter questions**

**49.1 Why is mesh popular?**  
It reinforces the wall without pulling the tissues tight. Less tension means less pain and a lower chance of the hernia coming back, in many adult groin hernias.

**49.2 What is the risk of mesh?**  
Infection of the mesh, chronic groin pain, and a feeling of stiffness. If the mesh is infected, it may need removal. So sterile technique and wound care matter.

### 50. What is a hiatus hernia, and what do you teach?

**Answer.**  
A hiatus hernia means part of the stomach slides up through the opening in the diaphragm into the chest. It is not a lump in the groin. The person has heartburn, sour fluid in the mouth, pain after meals, and symptoms that get worse when lying flat. A para-oesophageal hiatus hernia can twist and cut off blood supply. Severe chest or upper abdominal pain with vomiting then needs an urgent surgeon.

Nursing advice for the common sliding type:

- Raise the head end of the bed.
- Give small frequent meals. Do not eat a heavy meal near bedtime.
- Stay upright for about 2 hours after food.
- Avoid tight clothes around the waist, smoking, alcohol, and foods that worsen the burn, if the doctor has listed them.
- Give antacid or acid-reducing medicine only as ordered.
- Tell the person to report black stool, vomiting of blood, or pain that is sudden and severe.

**Counter questions**

**50.1 How is this different from an inguinal hernia on the ward?**  
An inguinal hernia is a groin lump that may cough and reduce. A hiatus hernia has no groin lump. The problem is heartburn and reflux. You do not put a groin support belt on a hiatus hernia.

### 51. What special names of hernia does the examiner ask?

**Answer.**  
These are less common, but they are favourite viva names.

- **Richter's hernia:** only one side of the bowel wall is trapped. The person can still pass some stool, but that piece of bowel can die. Pain and a tender lump still need the surgeon.
- **Littre's hernia:** the sac contains a Meckel's diverticulum, which is a small pouch left on the small bowel from before birth.
- **Maydl's hernia:** a W-shaped loop of bowel is in the sac. The middle loop can lose its blood supply inside the abdomen, so the outer lump may look less sick than the patient is.
- **Sliding hernia:** part of the wall of the sac is an organ, such as the caecum or the bladder. Urine output after surgery matters.
- **Pantaloon hernia:** direct and indirect inguinal hernias are present on the same side.

Your nursing action does not change with the name. A tense, painful, irreducible hernia is an emergency. Keep the patient nil by mouth and call the surgeon. Do not force it back.

**Counter questions**

**51.1 Why should you not be reassured if the patient with a Richter's hernia has passed stool?**  
Because the whole width of the bowel is not blocked. A dead patch of bowel wall can still be present. Ongoing pain, a tender lump, and a fast pulse still need urgent review.

### 52. What is the pre-operative nursing care for a planned hernia repair?

**Answer.**

- Explain the operation in simple words and check that consent is signed by the surgeon and the patient.
- Report cough, cold, fever, or constipation. These can postpone a planned operation.
- Weight, blood sugar, blood pressure, and medicines are checked. Ask about blood thinners.
- Tests often include haemoglobin, blood sugar, kidney tests, and an electrocardiogram if the patient is older. Electrocardiogram is shortened to ECG. Chest X-ray and viral markers are done if the hospital requires them.
- Skin preparation as per protocol. Do not shave in a way that cuts the skin the night before. Many hospitals clip hair in the theatre area on the day.
- Nil by mouth as ordered, often 6 to 8 hours for solid food. Follow the fasting instruction you are given. A small sip for an essential tablet may be allowed by the anaesthetist.
- Empty the bladder before shifting. A full bladder can be injured, especially in a sliding hernia, and it also makes the surgery harder.
- Mark the correct side. The patient can confirm the side.
- Give the pre-medication only when the order says, and then keep the patient in bed with side rails.
- Remove jewellery, dentures, and nail polish as per policy. Send the file, reports, and identity band with the patient.
- Teach deep breathing, ankle movement, and how to support the wound with a hand or a pillow when coughing after surgery.

**Counter questions**

**52.1 Why ankle movement and deep breathing?**  
Ankle movement reduces the chance of clots in the leg veins. Deep breathing reduces chest infection after anaesthesia.

**52.2 The patient ate breakfast by mistake. What do you do?**  
Do not hide it. Inform the anaesthetist and the surgeon at once. The operation may be postponed. Aspiration of food into the lungs during anaesthesia can kill.

### 53. What is the post-operative nursing care after hernia repair?

**Answer.**

- Receive the patient. Check airway, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse, the dressing, and the drain if any.
- Pain relief as ordered. A patient in severe pain cannot breathe deeply or pass urine.
- Watch the wound for soaking, swelling, or a growing bruise. A scrotal hematoma, which means a collection of blood inside the scrotum, can be large after groin hernia repair. Inform the surgeon if the scrotum is rapidly swelling or the patient is dizzy.
- Scrotal support and elevation help swelling and pain. Some surgeons order a scrotal support or a rolled towel support.
- Urine: ask the patient to pass urine within a few hours. Scan or feel for a full bladder if they cannot. Inform the doctor before the bladder overfills.
- Early walking as ordered, often the same day or the next day, reduces clots and chest problems.
- Food and drink when the patient is fully awake and the order allows. Start with sips.
- No heavy lifting for about 4 to 6 weeks, or as the surgeon says. No straining at stool. Give fluids, fibre, and a stool softener if ordered.
- Teach wound care: keep clean and dry, report fever, redness, pus, opening of the wound, or a new bulge.
- Men should report severe testicular pain or a sudden large scrotal swelling.

**Counter questions**

**53.1 What complications do you watch for?**  

- Retention of urine.
- Scrotal swelling and hematoma.
- Wound infection.
- Bleeding.
- Recurrence of the hernia.
- Chronic groin pain, sometimes from nerve irritation.
- Chest infection and leg vein clot, as after any operation.
- Mesh infection, which may appear as a wound that will not heal.
- Injury signs: inability to pass urine, or blood in urine, if the bladder was involved.

**53.2 When can the patient return to work?**  
Desk work may be possible in 1 to 2 weeks if the surgeon agrees. Heavy manual work usually waits about 4 to 6 weeks. The surgeon gives the exact time. Tell the patient not to compare with a neighbour.

**53.3 What do you teach about the dressing and the bath?**  
Follow the surgeon's instruction. Often the dressing stays dry for 48 hours. After that a shower may be allowed, not a long soak, until the wound is sealed. No powder or home paste on the wound.

### 54. What advice do you give before discharge?

**Answer.**

- Walk daily. Do not lift heavy weights, such as a water bucket, a gas cylinder, or a child, until the surgeon allows it.
- Support the wound if you cough.
- Eat fibre and drink water, unless a doctor has limited fluids. Prevent constipation.
- Stop smoking. A smoker's cough can bring the hernia back.
- Finish antibiotics only if they were prescribed. Many clean hernia repairs do not need a long antibiotic course. Do not demand extra antibiotics.
- Come back for the suture visit if sutures are present. Many meshes use absorbable skin stitches.
- Return at once if there is fever, pus, opening of the wound, vomiting, a tense painful new swelling, or no urine.

**Counter questions**

**54.1 Can the patient ride a bike next week?**  
Not unless the surgeon says so. Riding and heavy pedalling strain the repair. Walking is the usual early exercise.

## 5. Preeclampsia

### 55. What is preeclampsia?

**Answer.**  
Preeclampsia is a pregnancy problem. The textbook answer examiners expect is this: blood pressure of 140/90 millimetres of mercury or more, coming on after 20 weeks of pregnancy, with protein in the urine, in a woman who was previously normal.

It can also appear for the first time during labour, or in the first days after birth. It is a disease of the placenta and the mother's blood vessels. It can harm the mother's brain, liver, kidneys, and blood, and it can harm the baby.

Some newer guidelines also diagnose preeclampsia when there is high blood pressure after 20 weeks plus an organ problem, even if the urine protein is not yet positive. For the viva, start with high blood pressure plus protein in urine after 20 weeks. Then you can add that sentence if the examiner looks for an updated answer.

**Counter questions**

**55.1 What is gestational hypertension?**  
High blood pressure of 140/90 or more, starting after 20 weeks, without protein in the urine, and without the other organ problems of preeclampsia. It needs watching, because it can turn into preeclampsia.

**55.2 What is chronic hypertension in pregnancy?**  
High blood pressure that was present before pregnancy, or found before 20 weeks. It is not the same as preeclampsia, but preeclampsia can be added on top of it. That is called superimposed preeclampsia. Suspect it if protein newly appears, or if the blood pressure suddenly becomes much worse.

**55.3 What is eclampsia?**  
Eclampsia is preeclampsia with seizures. The seizures are not due to a known brain disease such as old epilepsy. Any fit in pregnancy after 20 weeks is treated as eclampsia until the doctor proves another cause.

**55.4 What is the only real cure?**  
Birth of the baby and delivery of the placenta. Medicines control the fits and the blood pressure until it is safe to deliver, and for a time after birth. Preeclampsia can still worsen in the first 48 hours after birth. Do not relax monitoring just because the baby is born.

### 56. What are the risk factors?

**Answer.**

- First pregnancy.
- Age under 20 or over 35, as often taught. Extremes of age.
- Twins or more.
- Diabetes, kidney disease, or chronic high blood pressure.
- Obesity.
- Preeclampsia in a previous pregnancy, or in the mother or sister.
- A new partner.
- Molar pregnancy or too much fluid, because the placenta is abnormal or the uterus is over-stretched.
- Autoimmune disease, if the examiner asks for a deeper answer.

**Counter questions**

**56.1 Does swelling of the feet mean preeclampsia?**  
No. Swelling of the feet is common in normal late pregnancy. Swelling of the face and hands, sudden weight gain, headache, and high blood pressure are more worrying. Preeclampsia can be present with little swelling. Do not wait for swollen feet to report a high blood pressure.

### 57. What are the danger signs?

**Answer.**  
Teach every antenatal mother these signs, and tell her to come the same day:

- Headache that does not go away.
- Blurred vision, flashing lights, or blindness.
- Pain in the upper abdomen, especially the right side under the ribs. This can mean the liver is involved.
- Vomiting in late pregnancy that is new.
- Sudden swelling of the face and hands.
- A fit, twitching, or loss of consciousness.
- Reduced baby movements.
- Bleeding from the vagina.
- Breathlessness.

These can mean impending eclampsia or severe disease. Impending eclampsia means a fit is likely soon.

**Counter questions**

**57.1 What will you find on examination in severe disease?**  

- Blood pressure 160/110 or more.
- Protein in urine 2+ or more on a dipstick, or a lot of protein in a 24-hour sample. The older teaching says 5 grams or more in 24 hours is severe.
- Brisk knee jerks and clonus. Clonus is repeated jerking when you flex the foot.
- Urine output less than 30 millilitres per hour, or less than 500 millilitres in 24 hours.
- Headache, visual changes, or upper abdominal pain.
- Lung crackles or breathlessness from fluid in the lungs.
- The baby may be small, and the fetal heart may be abnormal.

**57.2 What investigations are commonly sent?**  
Urine protein, complete blood count, platelet count, liver enzymes, kidney tests, and sometimes uric acid. A low platelet count and high liver enzymes are danger signs. Ultrasound for baby growth, and a non-stress test for the fetal heart, are often done. Non-stress test is shortened to NST. Eye check may be done if the blood pressure is very high.

### 58. What is HELLP syndrome?

**Answer.**  
HELLP is a severe form related to preeclampsia. The letters mean:

- **H:** Haemolysis. Red blood cells are breaking down.
- **EL:** Elevated Liver enzymes.
- **LP:** Low Platelet count.

The mother may have pain in the upper abdomen, nausea, a general feeling of being unwell, and a falling platelet count. The blood pressure may not always be extremely high, so do not ignore upper abdominal pain. HELLP can lead to bleeding, a liver tear, abruption of the placenta, and kidney failure. Treatment is in a higher centre. Delivery is planned by the obstetrician. Avoid unnecessary injections into the muscle if platelets are very low, because a hematoma can form. Magnesium sulphate is then often given through a vein, not into the muscle.

**Counter questions**

**58.1 What is abruption, in one line?**  
The placenta separates early, before the baby is born. The mother has pain, a hard tense uterus, and bleeding. The bleeding may be hidden inside. The baby can die, and the mother can develop a clotting failure. It is an emergency.

**58.2 What is DIC?**  
DIC means Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation. The body's clotting is used up, so the mother then bleeds from the gums, the wound, and the uterus, and blood does not clot. It can follow abruption, HELLP, a dead fetus that stays inside, or severe bleeding. Report oozing from the cannula site. Do not keep poking new veins without a plan.

### 59. What is mild preeclampsia, what is severe preeclampsia, and how much protein counts?

**Answer.**  
Protein in the urine that supports the diagnosis is about 300 milligrams or more in a 24-hour urine sample, a protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more, or a dipstick of 1+ or more when a better test is not available.

**Mild, or non-severe, preeclampsia**  
Blood pressure is 140/90 or more, but below 160/110. There is protein in the urine, but no severe symptoms. She can often be watched closely, sometimes in hospital, on the left side, with blood tests and a check of the baby.

**Severe preeclampsia**  
Any one of these makes it severe:

- Blood pressure 160/110 or more.
- Severe headache, visual change, or pain in the upper abdomen.
- Urine output below 30 millilitres per hour, or below 500 millilitres in 24 hours.
- Lung fluid or breathlessness.
- Platelets below 100,000.
- Liver enzymes rising, or kidney tests worsening.
- The baby is not growing, or the fetal heart is abnormal.

Severe disease needs admission, magnesium sulphate to prevent fits, blood-pressure medicine, and a delivery plan. Do not send her home with a headache and a blood pressure of 170/110.

**Counter questions**

**59.1 Should you restrict salt and protein?**  
Do not put her on a very low-salt diet. Older books said strict salt restriction. That can shrink her blood volume further. Give a normal hospital diet. Do not add extra salty snacks, and do not restrict protein unless the kidney doctor orders it.

**59.2 When can the fit happen?**  
Before birth, during labour, or after birth. Many fits are before delivery. A large share also happen in labour or in the first 48 hours after birth. This is why monitoring continues after the baby is born.

### 60. What complications can preeclampsia cause?

**Answer.**  
For the mother: eclampsia, stroke, blindness or visual loss, HELLP syndrome, bleeding behind the placenta, clotting failure, fluid in the lungs, kidney failure, and death if care is delayed.

For the baby: poor growth, too little oxygen, preterm birth, and stillbirth.

Tell the examiner that delivery is the cure, but the mother is still at risk for about 48 hours afterwards, and sometimes longer. Blood pressure can stay high for weeks and needs follow-up.

**Counter questions**

**60.1 She feels well except for a blood pressure of 150/96 and protein 1+. Can she go home from the clinic?**  
Not on your decision alone. This can still become severe quickly. Inform the doctor, repeat the blood pressure, check symptoms, and follow the clinic protocol. Teach the danger signs and a clear return plan if the doctor does allow outpatient watching.

### 61. What is the nursing care of a mother with preeclampsia?

**Answer.**  
Mild cases may be watched. Severe cases are admitted. Nursing care:

1. Admit in a quiet room, near the nurses' station. Dim light and less noise. Do not leave her alone if she has headache or brisk reflexes.
2. Bed rest in the left lateral position. Left side improves blood flow to the placenta and keeps the uterus off the big vein. She may sit up if she is breathless.
3. Check blood pressure as ordered, often every 15 to 30 minutes when she is severe, with the cuff of the right size. She should be rested, not talking, when you measure it.
4. Check urine protein. Keep a strict intake and output chart. Report urine below 30 millilitres per hour.
5. Daily weight, if she is stable enough to be weighed. Sudden gain suggests fluid retention.
6. Listen to the fetal heart as ordered. Ask about baby movements. Report a sudden drop in movements.
7. Seizure precautions: padded bed rails if available, suction ready, oxygen ready, airway ready, calcium gluconate ready if she is on magnesium sulphate. Do not put a spoon or a gag in the mouth during a fit.
8. Blood tests as ordered. Look at platelets before you assume an intramuscular injection is safe.
9. Give blood pressure medicines and magnesium sulphate only as ordered. Check the checks before each magnesium dose. Those checks are in the next question.
10. Limit fluids if the doctor orders it. In severe preeclampsia, too much fluid can flood the lungs. A common teaching figure is about 80 millilitres per hour total fluid, but follow the written order.
11. Prepare for delivery when the obstetrician decides. Give steroid injections for the baby's lungs only if ordered, and only if the pregnancy is preterm and the mother is stable enough to wait.
12. After birth, continue the same monitoring. Most fits occur during pregnancy or during labour, but they also occur after birth. Keep her in a closely observed area.

Explain the plan to the mother in simple words: "Your blood pressure is high. We are giving medicine so you do not fit. The baby will be delivered when it is safest. Please lie on your left side and tell me at once if your head hurts or your eyes are blurred."

**Counter questions**

**61.1 What is the left lateral position?**  
She lies on her left side. You can put a pillow under the abdomen and between the knees. Do not lay a heavily pregnant woman flat on her back for a long time. The uterus presses the big vein, the blood pressure falls, and the baby gets less blood. This is supine hypotension.

**61.2 Which blood pressure medicines are used, and which are avoided?**  
Used, when the doctor orders them:

- Labetalol, by mouth or into the vein, for high blood pressure. Be careful if she has asthma or a heart block. The doctor decides.
- Nifedipine, by mouth. Do not give it under the tongue. Under the tongue can drop the blood pressure too fast.
- Hydralazine into the vein, in some hospitals.
- Methyldopa, more for longer control, not for a sudden very high reading. It can cause sleepiness.

Avoid these in pregnancy: angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers. In simple words, the blood pressure tablets ending in "-pril" or "-sartan" are not for pregnancy. Do not give a diuretic water tablet as the first treatment of preeclampsia. The mother is often low in volume inside the vessels, even if her feet are swollen.

**61.3 What is the steroid for the baby's lungs?**  
If the baby is preterm and delivery can safely wait, the doctor may order betamethasone or dexamethasone by injection into the muscle. A common interview answer is betamethasone 12 milligrams intramuscularly, two doses, 24 hours apart. This helps the baby's lungs. It does not treat the mother's blood pressure. Do not delay an emergency delivery for a steroid if the mother or baby is unstable.

### 62. How do you give magnesium sulphate? The Pritchard regimen.

**Answer.**  
Magnesium sulphate is the medicine used to prevent and to treat fits in severe preeclampsia and eclampsia. The regimen examiners in India usually ask is the Pritchard regimen. It uses a vein dose and deep muscle doses.

**Loading dose**

- 4 grams intravenously. This is often prepared as 20 millilitres of 20 percent magnesium sulphate, given slowly, over about 5 minutes, not as a sudden push.
- Plus 10 grams into the muscle: 5 grams of 50 percent solution into each buttock, deep intramuscularly. That is 10 millilitres of 50 percent solution on each side.
- Many protocols add 1 millilitre of 2 percent lignocaine to each intramuscular injection to reduce pain. Follow your labour-room protocol.

**If a fit occurs again after the loading dose**

- An extra 2 grams intravenously, slowly, may be ordered. Call the doctor. Do not keep giving extra doses on your own.

**Maintenance dose**

- 5 grams of 50 percent magnesium sulphate deep into the muscle, every 4 hours, in alternate buttocks.
- Continue for 24 hours after the birth, or for 24 hours after the last fit, whichever comes later. Follow the doctor's order.

Keep calcium gluconate at the bedside before you start. The antidote is 10 millilitres of 10 percent calcium gluconate, given intravenously slowly over about 10 minutes, if there is magnesium toxicity.

There is also an intravenous method called the Zuspan regimen. A common form is 4 grams intravenously as a loading dose over about 15 to 20 minutes, then 1 to 2 grams per hour as an infusion. Some doctors use 6 grams, then 2 grams per hour. The nurse must use an infusion pump or a carefully counted drip, and check the mother often. Do not speed the drip.

**Counter questions**

**62.1 What do you check before every maintenance dose?**  
Do not give the next dose if any one of these is present. Call the doctor.

- Respiratory rate below 16 breaths per minute.
- Knee jerk is absent. Loss of the knee jerk is the earliest clinical sign of too much magnesium.
- Urine output below 30 millilitres per hour, or below 100 millilitres in the previous 4 hours. Magnesium leaves the body through the kidneys. If urine is poor, magnesium builds up.

Also check that the baby has a heart beat if she is still pregnant, and check the mother's consciousness.

**62.2 What are the signs of magnesium toxicity, from early to late?**  
Loss of knee jerk, then drowsiness, then slow breathing, then breathing stops, then the heart can stop. Flushing and warmth after the loading dose are common and are not the same as toxicity. Still watch the breathing.

**62.3 What do you do if breathing becomes very slow?**  
Stop magnesium. Call for help. Give oxygen. Support breathing with a bag-mask if needed. Give calcium gluconate intravenously as ordered, 10 millilitres of 10 percent, slowly. Do not leave the mother. Inform the doctor. Continue to watch the uterus, because stopping magnesium does not stop the need to watch for fits and bleeding.

**62.4 Why must the urine output be good?**  
The kidneys excrete magnesium. In preeclampsia the kidneys may be failing. A maintenance dose given to a mother who has not passed urine can stop her breathing.

**62.5 Can magnesium and nifedipine be given together?**  
Both can lower the blood pressure. Some mothers receive both, but only with a doctor's order and with blood pressure checks. Do not add nifedipine on your own because the blood pressure is high. Call the doctor. Also do not give magnesium as a fast intravenous push. A fast push can stop the heart.

**62.6 What is the knee jerk?**  
Ask her to sit or lie with the knee bent and relaxed. Tap the tendon just below the knee cap. The leg kicks slightly if the reflex is present. Absent reflex means stop and call. Very brisk reflex with clonus means she is at risk of a fit. That is a reason she needs magnesium, not a reason to stop it. Learn the difference: absent means toxicity. Very brisk means disease activity.

**62.7 What is the low-dose or Dhaka regimen?**  
Some Indian hospitals use a smaller dose for smaller women. One low-dose method often asked is 4 grams slow intravenous as loading, then 2 grams slow intravenous every 3 hours. The Dhaka method often taught is 4 grams intravenous plus 3 grams into each buttock as loading, then 2.5 grams into the muscle every 4 hours. Say the Pritchard regimen first. Mention the low-dose method only if the examiner asks, and follow the protocol written in your labour room.

### 63. What do you do if the mother has a fit?

**Answer.**

1. Shout for help. Note the time the fit starts.
2. Do not force a spoon, a mouth gag, or your finger into the mouth.
3. Turn her onto her left side. Protect her head. Do not tightly restrain the arms and legs. Stop her from falling off the bed.
4. After the jerking, open the airway, suction if secretions are present, and give oxygen.
5. Call the obstetrician and the emergency team.
6. Magnesium sulphate loading dose is started as ordered if she is not already on it.
7. Check blood pressure, pulse, and the fetal heart after the fit.
8. Look for injury, tongue bite, and start of labour or bleeding. A fit can be followed by abruption.
9. Keep her nil by mouth until she is fully awake and the doctor allows sips.
10. Prepare for delivery as the obstetrician decides. Stay with her. One fit can be followed by another.
11. After the event, write the time, the length of the fit, the medicines, the fetal heart, and the mother's response.

Most eclamptic fits stop on their own within a minute or two. Your job during the jerking is protection and calling for help, not pouring medicine into the mouth.

**Counter questions**

**63.1 How is this different from an epileptic fit?**  
You may not know at the bedside. Treat a pregnant woman after 20 weeks who fits as eclampsia until proven otherwise. Check blood pressure and urine protein. A past history of epilepsy matters, but preeclampsia can still be present. Do not give only an epilepsy tablet and walk away.

**63.2 The fetal heart is very slow after the fit. What do you do?**  
Give the mother oxygen, keep her on the left side, call the doctor at once, and prepare for possible emergency delivery. Do not wait for the next scheduled fetal heart check.

**63.3 She is in the toilet and fits. What do you do?**  
Protect the head, call for help, do not drag her roughly, and move her to a safe flat place as soon as help arrives. Then follow the same steps. This is why a severe preeclampsia mother should not walk alone to a far toilet. Offer a bedpan or stay close.

## 6. PPH (Postpartum Haemorrhage)

PPH means Postpartum Haemorrhage. Postpartum means after birth. Haemorrhage means heavy bleeding.

### 64. What is PPH?

**Answer.**  
PPH is heavy bleeding after the birth of the baby. The amounts examiners still expect you to say first:

- 500 millilitres or more after a vaginal birth.
- 1000 millilitres or more after a caesarean birth.

Also call it PPH if the bleeding is less than that number but the mother has signs of shock, such as a fast pulse, low blood pressure, sweating, or feeling faint. A 2025 World Health Organization update says to act even earlier: if blood loss reaches about 300 millilitres and the vital signs are becoming abnormal. Do not wait for a full 500 millilitres if she is already pale and her pulse is rising. A calibrated blood-collection drape, if your labour room has one, measures the loss better than guessing.

**Primary PPH** is bleeding within 24 hours of birth. Most of it happens in the first hour. That first hour is part of the fourth stage of labour, and it is why the nurse stays close.

**Secondary PPH** is heavy bleeding from 24 hours after birth up to 6 weeks. The World Health Organization extends this window up to 12 weeks. Causes of secondary PPH include a bit of placenta left inside, infection of the uterus, and poor involution. Involution means the uterus shrinking back.

**Minor PPH** is about 500 to 1000 millilitres. **Major PPH** is more than 1000 millilitres. Blood loss more than 2000 millilitres is often called severe. Any of these is an emergency if she shows shock.

**Counter questions**

**64.1 Why is the first hour so important?**  
The placenta has just separated and left a raw area. If the uterus stays hard, the muscle fibres close the blood vessels. If the uterus relaxes, those vessels bleed. Most mothers who die from PPH die from delay, not from a lack of a rare operation. Early massage, early oxytocin, and early call for help save life.

**64.2 How do you measure blood loss better?**  
Weigh the pads. One gram of weight is about one millilitre of blood, after you subtract the dry pad weight. Count clots. Look under the buttocks. Blood hides in the sheet. Record the total. Do not write "moderate" without a number if your chart asks for an amount.

### 65. What are the four causes of PPH?

**Answer.**  
Remember the four Ts:

1. **Tone.** The uterus is soft and will not contract. This is uterine atony. It is the most common cause, about 70 to 80 percent. A soft uterus is called boggy.
2. **Trauma.** A tear of the perineum, vagina, or cervix, a ruptured uterus, or an inverted uterus. The uterus may be hard, but bleeding continues.
3. **Tissue.** Placenta or membranes are left inside, or the placenta has not come out. The uterus cannot contract fully.
4. **Thrombin.** A clotting problem. Blood does not clot. Causes include abruption, HELLP, severe preeclampsia, a dead fetus retained inside, amniotic fluid entering the mother's blood (a rare emergency called amniotic fluid embolism), or a known bleeding disorder.

A full bladder is not a fifth T, but it is a very common reason the uterus will not stay hard. Always check the bladder.

**Counter questions**

**65.1 How do you tell tone from trauma at the bedside?**  
Feel the uterus. If it is soft, large, and the bleeding is heavy, think tone. Massage it. If the uterus is hard and well contracted and the bleeding is still bright red, think trauma, often a cervical or vaginal tear. Call the doctor to examine with a good light. Do not keep massaging a hard uterus and ignore a tear.

**65.2 What are the risk factors?**  

- A long labour, or a very fast labour.
- A uterus that was over-stretched: twins, a big baby, or too much liquor.
- Many previous births. The muscle is weaker.
- Induction or a long oxytocin drip.
- Infection of the uterus during labour.
- Previous PPH.
- Placenta previa, or the placenta stuck abnormally.
- Abruption.
- Anaemia. She has less reserve. A smaller loss can kill her.
- Full bladder.
- Instrumental delivery, which raises the chance of tears.
- A fibroid, which is a muscle lump in the wall of the uterus.
- General anaesthesia, which can relax the uterus.
- Bleeding disorder, or a mother on blood thinners.

**65.3 What is uterine inversion?**  
The uterus turns inside out. It can happen if the cord is pulled hard when the uterus is soft, or if the placenta is attached at the fundus and is pulled. The fundus is the top of the uterus. The mother collapses. The shock can be worse than the visible blood. You may see a red mass at the vagina, and you may not feel the fundus in the abdomen. Do not keep pulling the cord. Call the doctor at once. The doctor replaces the uterus. Stop oxytocin until the uterus is back in place, then start it so the uterus stays contracted. This is a doctor's procedure. The nurse calls, opens a vein, gives oxygen, and does not pull.

### 66. What is AMTSL?

**Answer.**  
AMTSL means Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour. It prevents PPH. It has three parts:

1. A uterotonic medicine just after the baby is born. Uterotonic means a medicine that makes the uterus contract. The usual medicine is oxytocin 10 international units into the muscle, within one minute of birth, if the mother has no reason to avoid it. International units are shortened to IU.
2. Controlled cord traction to deliver the placenta, done only by a skilled person, and only when the uterus is contracted and there are signs that the placenta has separated.
3. Uterine massage after the placenta is out, until the uterus stays hard.

Delayed cord clamping, for about 1 to 3 minutes, is good for the baby if the baby is crying and well. The oxytocin can still be given. Do not delay the medicine if the mother is already bleeding.

**Counter questions**

**66.1 What is controlled cord traction?**  
One hand supports the uterus and pushes it slightly upward, so you do not invert it. The other hand gives steady downward traction on the cord during a contraction. You do not yank. If the placenta does not come, you wait for the next contraction. If you are a new nurse and you are not signed off for this skill, you call the skilled midwife or the doctor. You still give the oxytocin and you still massage once the placenta is out, as per your role.

**66.2 What if there is no oxytocin?**  
The doctor may order misoprostol. A prevention dose often taught is 600 micrograms by mouth. For treatment of bleeding, 800 micrograms is the usual interview answer. Follow the protocol on your trolley.

### 67. What do you do when the mother is bleeding heavily?

**Answer.**  
Say this in order. Examiners want the order.

1. Call for help. Do not leave her alone. Pull the emergency bell.
2. Rub up a contraction. Massage the fundus with a cupped hand until it is hard. If clots are inside, the doctor may expel them by supporting the uterus. Do not push hard on a soft uterus in a way that causes inversion.
3. Airway and oxygen. Lay her flat. If she is not breathless, you may raise the legs. Keep her warm.
4. Open two wide vein lines if you are trained. A wide cannula is often size 16 or 18. A lower gauge number means a wider tube, so blood and fluid can run fast. Send blood for haemoglobin, clotting, and cross-match. Cross-match means the blood bank checks that the donor blood matches her blood. Start fluids as ordered. Ringer's lactate is commonly used while blood is coming.
5. Empty the bladder with a catheter.
6. Check whether the placenta is out. If it is out, check that it is complete. If it is not out, tell the doctor. Do not pull a cord on a soft uterus.
7. Give uterotonic medicines as ordered. Oxytocin is first. See the medicine question below.
8. Estimate and record blood loss, pulse, and blood pressure every 5 to 15 minutes.
9. If the uterus is hard and bleeding continues, prepare for examination of tears in the theatre or the labour room as the doctor decides.
10. If medicines fail, prepare for balloon tamponade, and then theatre for surgical control. Surgical options include compression stitches, tying of vessels, and, as a last step, removal of the uterus. The nurse prepares, communicates, and continues resuscitation.
11. Keep the family informed through a responsible staff member. Record everything.

**Counter questions**

**67.1 What is bimanual compression?**  
A skilled doctor or midwife puts one hand in the vagina and the other on the abdomen and squeezes the uterus between the hands to stop bleeding until medicines or theatre can work. It is painful. Explain it. Do not attempt it if you have not been trained.

**67.2 What is aortic compression?**  
A temporary measure. A fist presses the aorta through the abdominal wall, above the uterus, against the spine. It reduces bleeding while help arrives. It is not a cure. The person must be trained.

**67.3 What is balloon tamponade?**  
A balloon, such as a Bakri balloon, or a condom tied on a tube, is placed inside the uterus and filled with sterile fluid, often a few hundred millilitres, until bleeding slows. It stays for several hours as the doctor orders, then is removed in a controlled way. The nurse watches bleeding, pain, fundal height, and vital signs. A sudden return of bleeding is reported at once.

**67.4 What blood do you arrange?**  
The doctor decides. Cross-matched blood is best. If the mother is collapsing and there is no time to wait, the hospital massive-bleeding protocol may release O negative blood before the full match is ready. O negative is the emergency blood group used in that situation. You send the sample early. The common failure is sending the sample late. Also ask for clotting products if the doctor orders them, such as fresh frozen plasma, which is the liquid part of blood that helps clotting.

### 68. Which medicines are used for PPH, and when is each avoided?

**Answer.**  
Interview doses. Give them only on a doctor's order and as written in your labour-room protocol.

**Oxytocin**

- Prevention: 10 IU intramuscularly after the baby is born.
- Treatment: 10 IU intramuscularly if not already given, and an infusion such as 20 to 40 units in 500 to 1000 millilitres of Ringer's lactate or normal saline, run fast until the uterus is hard, then slowed to a rate that keeps it hard.
- Do not push a large amount of undiluted oxytocin quickly into the vein. A fast intravenous bolus can drop the blood pressure.
- Oxytocin is the first medicine for most mothers, including mothers with high blood pressure.

**Misoprostol**

- Treatment dose often asked: 800 micrograms under the tongue, or 800 to 1000 micrograms in the rectum.
- Useful when a vein line is not yet in, and in places where oxytocin is not ready.
- Side effects: shivering and fever. Warn the mother so she is not frightened.

**Methylergometrine, also called methergine**

- 0.2 milligrams intramuscularly.
- It causes a strong, lasting contraction.
- Do not give it in high blood pressure, preeclampsia, eclampsia, or heart disease. It can cause a dangerous rise in blood pressure and spasm of vessels.
- This is a favourite counter question. If the mother has preeclampsia and PPH, say oxytocin and misoprostol. Do not say methergine.

**Carboprost**

- This is 15-methyl prostaglandin F2 alpha. A common brand teaching name is Prostodin.
- 250 micrograms deep intramuscularly. It can be repeated about every 15 minutes. The maximum often taught is 8 doses, which is 2 milligrams.
- Do not give it in asthma. It can tighten the airways and make breathing very hard. Also use caution in heart, kidney, or liver disease. The doctor decides.
- Side effects: diarrhoea, vomiting, and fever.

**Tranexamic acid**

- It helps the clot stay in place. It does not contract the uterus. It is given along with oxytocin, not instead of oxytocin.
- Interview dose: 1 gram intravenously over about 10 minutes, as soon as possible, within 3 hours of birth. A second 1 gram may be given if bleeding continues after 30 minutes, or if it restarts within 24 hours.
- It is less useful if given late, after 3 hours. Do not delay oxytocin in order to prepare tranexamic acid. Do both.

**Counter questions**

**68.1 The mother has asthma and is bleeding. Which medicine do you question?**  
Question carboprost. Tell the doctor she has asthma. Oxytocin remains first line.

**68.2 The mother had preeclampsia. Which medicine do you question?**  
Question methylergometrine. Say so clearly before you draw it up. Many trolleys keep it next to oxytocin. Check the label.

**68.3 Can you give all of these together?**  
The doctor may add a second and a third medicine if the uterus stays soft. You give each only when it is ordered. You say the name aloud. You do not mix everything in one syringe.

### 69. What is the MOTIVE bundle?

**Answer.**  
MOTIVE is the first-response bundle taught in the 2025 World Health Organization postpartum haemorrhage guidance. Say the letters, then the action.

- **M:** Massage the uterus.
- **O:** Oxytocic medicine, usually oxytocin.
- **T:** Tranexamic acid, 1 gram into the vein within 3 hours of birth.
- **I:** Intravenous fluids through a wide cannula.
- **V:** Vaginal and cervical examination by a skilled person, to look for tears.
- **E:** Escalate. Call a senior doctor, move to theatre, or refer if bleeding continues.

Do these together, not one by one with long gaps. Massage and the call for help still come in the first seconds, even before the bundle name is spoken.

**Counter questions**

**69.1 What is carbetocin?**  
Carbetocin is a long-acting uterotonic used for prevention, as a single dose of 100 micrograms into the muscle or slowly into the vein. A heat-stable form is useful where oxytocin cannot be kept cold. It does not replace the MOTIVE steps if she is already bleeding heavily. Most Indian labour rooms still use oxytocin 10 international units for prevention. Follow your trolley.

### 70. What are the degrees of a perineal tear?

**Answer.**  
A perineal tear is a tear of the birth outlet. Examiners ask four degrees.

1. **First degree:** skin only.
2. **Second degree:** skin and perineal muscles. The muscle around the anus is intact.
3. **Third degree:** the anal sphincter muscle is torn. This is the muscle that keeps stool in.
4. **Fourth degree:** the tear goes through the sphincter into the rectal lining.

First and second degree tears may be repaired in the labour room by a skilled person, with pain relief. Third and fourth degree tears need a skilled repair, often in the theatre, with good light and anaesthesia. The nurse reports severe perineal pain, bleeding, or stool or flatus coming through the vagina. Give a stool softener as ordered, perineal wash, and antibiotics if ordered. Do not do a rectal examination roughly.

**Counter questions**

**70.1 The uterus is hard, but blood keeps coming from the perineum. What do you think?**  
Think trauma, not atony. Look at the tear or the episiotomy with a good light, press a clean pad, and call the doctor to repair it. Continuing to massage a hard uterus will not close a tear.

### 71. What is a retained placenta?

**Answer.**  
The placenta is retained if it has not been delivered within 30 minutes when active management is used. Sometimes a placenta comes out but a piece is missing. Both can cause bleeding.

Nurse's actions:

- Call the doctor. Do not keep pulling the cord.
- Empty the bladder.
- Make sure oxytocin has been given as ordered.
- If the uterus is soft, do not do cord traction.
- Keep a vein line, send cross-match, and watch the pulse and blood loss.
- Prepare the theatre if manual removal is planned. Manual removal is the doctor separating the placenta with a hand, under anaesthesia.
- After a piece is removed, continue oxytocin and watch for bleeding and infection.

A placenta that is abnormally stuck, called placenta accreta, may not separate at all. Pulling harder can tear the uterus or turn it inside out. Stop pulling and call the senior obstetrician.

**Counter questions**

**71.1 The placenta looks complete, but she bleeds an hour later. Is retained tissue still possible?**  
Yes. A small piece or a membrane can be missed. Also think of a soft uterus, a tear, or a full bladder. Massage, check the bladder, call the doctor, and do not assume the earlier check was perfect.

### 72. What is the nursing care after bleeding is controlled?

**Answer.**

- Stay with her. Check pulse, blood pressure, uterus, and pad every 15 minutes until stable, then as ordered.
- The uterus should be hard, in the midline, and at or below the umbilicus. If it rises or becomes soft, massage and call.
- Urine output at least 30 millilitres per hour. She may still have a catheter.
- Oxygen if saturation is low. Keep her warm. A cold mother does not clot well.
- Blood transfusion care if blood is running. Watch for reaction.
- Check the perineal pad and under the buttocks. Hidden bleeding continues in the bed.
- Haemoglobin is repeated as ordered. She may need iron later.
- Explain what happened in simple words when she is awake. Mothers often feel terrified.
- Breastfeeding can continue when she is stable. It releases natural oxytocin and helps the uterus stay contracted. Help her latch when she is able. Do not force it during shock.
- Before discharge, teach her the signs of secondary PPH: large clots, soaking a pad in an hour, foul discharge, fever, or feeling faint. She must return the same day.

**Counter questions**

**72.1 What is secondary PPH care?**  
Admit her. Vital signs. Vein line. Blood tests. The doctor orders antibiotics if infection is suspected, and an ultrasound to look for retained pieces. Oxytocin may be ordered. She may need evacuation of the uterus in theatre. Do not do a vaginal examination roughly on the ward. Support breastfeeding or milk expression if they are separated. This mother can bleed again. Keep her near the station.

**72.2 What is lochia, and when is it too much?**  
Lochia is the normal discharge after birth.

- Lochia rubra: red, for about the first 3 to 4 days.
- Lochia serosa: pink or brown, up to about 10 days.
- Lochia alba: white or yellow-white, up to about 3 to 6 weeks.

Too much: soaking a pad in one hour, large clots, a sudden gush after the bleeding had settled, or a foul smell. Foul smell suggests infection. Report both.

## 7. Stages of labour

Labour is the process by which the baby, the placenta, and the membranes leave the uterus. Normal labour is at term, starts on its own, the baby is head down, and both mother and baby stay well.

### 73. What is the difference between true labour and false labour?

**Answer.**  
False labour pains are Braxton Hicks contractions. They prepare the uterus but they do not open the cervix in a progressive way.

**True labour**

- Contractions come at regular intervals.
- They become closer, longer, and stronger.
- Pain often starts in the lower back and moves to the front.
- Walking does not stop them.
- There is "show." Show is a mucus plug mixed with a small amount of blood.
- The cervix dilates and effaces. Dilatation means opening. Effacement means thinning.

**False labour**

- Contractions are irregular.
- They do not get steadily stronger.
- Pain is mostly in the front.
- Rest, a walk, or a change of position may ease them.
- There is no progressive opening of the cervix.
- Show is usually absent.

Only a cervical check by a trained person can confirm labour. Do not send a mother home if she is bleeding fresh blood, if the baby is not moving, if the cord is felt, or if she has a headache with high blood pressure.

**Counter questions**

**73.1 What is show, and how is it different from dangerous bleeding?**  
Show is sticky mucus with streaks of blood. It can appear as the cervix starts to open. Dangerous bleeding is fresh bright blood, soaking, with or without pain. In late pregnancy, fresh bleeding can mean placenta previa or abruption. Placenta previa means the placenta is lying low and covering, or partly covering, the cervix. Do not do a vaginal examination in antepartum bleeding until placenta previa is ruled out by ultrasound. Antepartum means before birth. A vaginal examination can cause massive bleeding if the placenta covers the cervix.

**73.2 What do you note when the membranes rupture?**  
Note the time, the colour, the amount, and the smell.

- Clear or pale straw fluid is usual.
- Green or thick fluid means meconium. Meconium is the baby's first stool. If it is passed into the fluid before birth, the baby may be under stress. Tell the doctor. Prepare for newborn resuscitation.
- Blood-stained fluid needs an urgent doctor review.
- A foul smell suggests infection.

Check the fetal heart at once after the waters break. A cord can come down with the water. This is cord prolapse. Then check the mother's temperature later, because prolonged rupture raises infection risk. Tell her not to insert anything into the vagina. Give a sterile pad.

### 74. What are the four stages of labour?

**Answer.**

**First stage**  
From the start of true labour until the cervix is fully open, which is 10 centimetres.

- Latent phase: the cervix opens slowly, from closed to about 4 centimetres. Contractions are milder.
- Active phase: from about 4 centimetres to 10 centimetres. Contractions are stronger and closer.

Some newer guidelines say the active phase starts nearer 5 or 6 centimetres. For most Indian hospital vivas, say latent phase up to about 4 centimetres, and active phase from 4 centimetres to full dilatation. You can add the newer point if asked about recent changes.

Average duration often taught: about 12 hours in a first-time mother, and about 6 to 8 hours in a mother who has given birth before. These are averages, not deadlines. The partograph shows whether this mother is progressing.

**Second stage**  
From full dilatation until the baby is born.

- It has a passive part, when the head comes down without strong pushing, and an active part, when the mother pushes.
- Often taught duration: up to about 2 hours in a first birth, and about 1 hour in a later birth. With an epidural, it may be longer. An epidural is a pain-relief injection given in the lower back by an anaesthetist. The doctor decides if the second stage is too long. Do not make her push for hours if the baby is in distress.

**Third stage**  
From the birth of the baby until the placenta and membranes are out. Usually 5 to 15 minutes with active management. It is prolonged if the placenta is not out by 30 minutes of active management. Then the doctor must see her.

**Fourth stage**  
The first 1 to 2 hours after the placenta is out. This is the high-risk hour for bleeding, even though many textbooks call labour three stages and then add this as the fourth. Watch the mother. Do not send her to the postnatal ward the minute the placenta is out.

**Counter questions**

**74.1 What is a contraction pattern you expect in active labour?**  
About 3 to 5 contractions in 10 minutes, each lasting about 40 to 60 seconds, and strong. Weak, few contractions may mean poor progress. Very frequent contractions, with little rest, can exhaust the baby. Report a uterus that does not relax between pains. That can mean too much oxytocin or abruption.

**74.2 What is effacement and station?**  
Effacement is how thin the cervix is, often written as a percentage. Station is how far down the presenting part is, compared with the ischial spines. The presenting part is the part of the baby that is coming first, usually the head. The ischial spines are bony points felt on the side walls of the pelvis. Zero station means the head is at the spines. Minus numbers are above the spines. Plus numbers are below the spines. Plus 2 or plus 3 means the head is low. Crowning is when the head no longer slips back between contractions, and the widest part distends the vulva.

### 75. What is your nursing care in the first stage?

**Answer.**

- Welcome her. Check the antenatal card: blood group, haemoglobin, blood pressure history, HIV and hepatitis status as recorded, allergies, and the expected date.
- Vital signs. Blood pressure every 2 to 4 hours if normal, more often if high. Temperature every 4 hours, more often if the waters have been broken for long. Pulse more often in active labour.
- Fetal heart: every 30 minutes in the latent phase and every 15 minutes in the active phase is a common teaching schedule. More often if the water is meconium, if oxytocin is running, or if the heart rate was abnormal. Listen just after a contraction for a full minute.
- Contractions: frequency, duration, and strength, by a hand resting on the fundus. Do not press hard during the pain.
- Vaginal examination only as needed, with consent, privacy, and aseptic technique. Too many examinations increase infection. Record dilatation, effacement, station, membranes, and the presenting part.
- Empty the bladder often. A full bladder slows labour and increases bleeding later.
- Position: let her walk in early labour if the membranes are intact and the head is engaged and there is no bleeding. Later, left lateral or an upright position is good. Avoid long periods flat on the back.
- Fluids and light food in early labour if the hospital allows and if caesarean birth is not expected soon. In active labour, many hospitals allow clear fluids. Follow the protocol.
- Pain support: explain, stay with her, back massage, breathing, and a cool cloth. Medicines such as an epidural, or pethidine which is a strong pain injection, only if ordered. Pethidine can make the baby slow to breathe if given close to birth. Tell the doctor if birth seems near.
- Start or update the partograph in the active phase.
- Watch for danger: fresh bleeding, a cord, a very slow or very fast fetal heart, meconium, a high blood pressure, a fit, a scar tenderness in a mother with a previous caesarean, and a uterus that does not relax.
- Emotional care: introduce yourself, explain each check, and do not threaten her with words like "you will rupture" in order to make her quiet.

**Counter questions**

**75.1 What is a partograph?**  
A partograph is a graph of the labour. It shows cervical dilatation, descent of the head, contractions, the fetal heart, the mother's pulse and blood pressure, temperature, urine, and medicines such as oxytocin. Plotting usually starts in the active phase at about 4 centimetres. The alert line expects about 1 centimetre of opening per hour. The action line is 4 hours to the right of the alert line. If the plot crosses the alert line, inform the doctor and watch closely. If it reaches the action line, she needs a decision: stronger contractions, or delivery by another method. Do not leave a crossed action line for the next shift.

**75.2 What are the three Ps if labour is slow?**  
Power, passage, and passenger.

- Power: weak contractions.
- Passage: the pelvis or the soft tissues are not allowing descent.
- Passenger: the baby is big, or the position is abnormal, such as a brow, or the baby is not head down.

The nurse reports the findings. The doctor decides the action.

**75.3 What is oxytocin care during labour?**  
Oxytocin is a high-alert infusion. Check the concentration, use a pump if available, increase only as ordered, and record the fetal heart and contractions. Stop the oxytocin and call the doctor if the fetal heart stays slow, if contractions are too close with no rest, or if there is scar pain or heavy bleeding. Stay with a mother on an oxytocin drip.

### 76. What is the mechanism of labour?

**Answer.**  
The mechanism is the series of movements the baby's head makes to fit through the pelvis. For a normal head-first birth, say these steps:

1. **Engagement.** The widest part of the head has entered the pelvis. On abdominal exam, the head is fixed and not easily moved.
2. **Descent.** The head comes down, helped by contractions and, later, by pushing.
3. **Flexion.** The chin tucks onto the chest, so a smaller diameter leads.
4. **Internal rotation.** The back of the head turns to the front, under the pubic bone.
5. **Crowning.** The head stays visible between contractions.
6. **Extension.** The head is born by extending, with the face passing over the perineum.
7. **Restitution.** The head turns slightly back toward the side it came from, as it realigns with the shoulders.
8. **External rotation.** The shoulders rotate inside, and the head turns further to the side.
9. **Expulsion.** The anterior shoulder, then the posterior shoulder, then the body is born.

You do not need to pull the baby out. Support the head and the perineum. Ask the mother for small breaths at crowning so the head comes slowly. This reduces tears.

**Counter questions**

**76.1 What is engagement in simple abdominal words?**  
If you can still feel a large part of the head above the pubic bone, it may not be engaged. In a first-time mother near term, an unengaged head needs a doctor review. In a mother who has delivered before, the head may engage only after labour starts.

**76.2 What is moulding and caput?**  
Moulding is overlap of the skull bones so the head can fit. A little moulding is normal. Severe moulding, graded as more overlap, can mean a long obstructed labour. Caput is a soft swelling of the scalp from pressure. It can confuse you into thinking the head is lower than it is. Both are recorded. Severe moulding with poor descent is a warning. Report it.

### 77. What is your nursing care in the second stage?

**Answer.**

- Confirm full dilatation before you ask her to bear down. Pushing on a cervix that is not fully open exhausts her and swells the cervix.
- Empty the bladder if it is full.
- Position: upright, side-lying, or the position she finds strongest, as per hospital practice. Support her so she does not fall.
- Fetal heart after every contraction, or at least every 5 minutes.
- Teach pushing: chin on chest, a breath, push as if opening the bowel, for the length of the contraction, then rest. Do not make her hold one breath for a very long time until she is purple, if coached pushing is not required. Follow the midwife leading the birth.
- At crowning, ask her to pant so the head is born slowly. Support the perineum with a clean pad. Support the head. Check for the cord around the neck. If the cord is loose, slip it over the head. If it is tight, the doctor or skilled midwife clamps and cuts it. Do not pull a tight cord.
- Note the time of birth.
- Dry the baby, stimulate, and place on the mother's abdomen for skin-to-skin if the baby is crying. Delay cord clamping for 1 to 3 minutes if the baby is well. Cover them with a dry towel. Put on a cap.
- If the baby is not crying, move to the resuscitation corner at once. Call for help. Do not wait to take a beautiful photograph.
- An episiotomy is a cut in the perineum. It is not routine. It is done by a skilled person if there is a clear reason, such as instrumental birth, a rigid perineum, or fetal distress when a few seconds matter. Pain relief for the cut is required. A mediolateral cut is the one usually taught in India, because a midline cut can extend into the rectum.

**Counter questions**

**77.1 What is Apgar score?**  
Apgar is a score of the baby's condition at 1 minute and at 5 minutes. The letters are a name, and also a memory aid:

- Appearance, which is colour: blue or pale is 0, body pink and limbs blue is 1, fully pink is 2.
- Pulse: absent is 0, below 100 is 1, 100 or more is 2.
- Grimace, which is response to stimulation: none is 0, a face or weak response is 1, a cry or cough is 2.
- Activity, which is tone: limp is 0, some flexion is 1, active movement is 2.
- Respiration: absent is 0, slow or irregular is 1, a good cry is 2.

The total is 10. A score of 7 to 10 is reassuring. 4 to 6 is moderately depressed. 0 to 3 is severely depressed. If the score is still low at 5 minutes, repeat it. Do not delay resuscitation in order to calculate the 1-minute score. If the baby is not breathing, start help immediately. The score is written after the care has started.

**77.2 What is the care of an episiotomy after birth?**  
Check it for bleeding and hematoma. A hematoma is a painful swelling of collected blood. Report severe perineal pain. Give perineal wash, a clean pad, and a stool softener. Teach her to pour water during urination so it does not sting. Watch REEDA: Redness, Edema which means swelling, Ecchymosis which means bruising, Discharge, and Approximation which means the edges are together.

### 78. What is your nursing care in the third stage?

**Answer.**

- Do not pull the cord as soon as the baby is out.
- Give oxytocin 10 IU intramuscularly for active management, as per protocol.
- Watch for signs that the placenta has separated:
  1. A gush of blood.
  2. The cord lengthens.
  3. The uterus becomes firm, round, and rises up in the abdomen.
  4. If you press above the pubic bone, the cord does not pull back.
- Once these signs are present and the uterus is contracted, a skilled person does controlled cord traction.
- Receive the placenta in both hands. Twist gently so the membranes come out whole. Do not tear them off roughly.
- Massage the uterus until it is hard.
- Examine the placenta.
  - Maternal surface: all cotyledons present. Cotyledons are the lobes. There are often about 15 to 20. A missing piece means retained tissue and a risk of bleeding. Tell the doctor.
  - Membranes: look for a hole that matches the rupture, and see that the rest is complete.
  - Cord: two arteries and one vein. A single artery is reported, because it can be linked with a baby problem. The paediatrician should know.
  - Look for abnormal smell, clots, or an extra lobe.
- Check the perineum, and the vagina if you are trained, for tears. If bleeding continues with a hard uterus, call the doctor to look at the cervix.
- If the placenta is not out by 30 minutes, call the doctor. Keep the bladder empty. Do not keep pulling. She may need manual removal in the theatre under anaesthesia. Manual removal means the doctor inserts a hand and separates the placenta. The nurse prepares theatre, a vein line, cross-match, and oxytocin for after removal.

**Counter questions**

**78.1 What is Schultz and what is Duncan delivery of the placenta?**  
Schultz means the shiny fetal side appears first. Separation started in the centre. Bleeding is often less. Duncan means the rough maternal side appears first. Separation started at the edge. Bleeding is often more. Both can be normal if the placenta is complete and the uterus stays hard. Duncan type needs a closer eye on blood loss.

**78.2 What is Brandt-Andrews method?**  
It is controlled cord traction with the other hand guarding the uterus by pushing it up. It prevents inversion. It is done only after separation, and only by a trained person.

**78.3 The placenta is complete but she bleeds. What now?**  
This is PPH care. Massage, oxytocin infusion, empty the bladder, oxygen, two vein lines, and call for help. A complete placenta does not rule out atony or a tear.

### 79. What is your nursing care in the fourth stage?

**Answer.**  
This hour saves mothers. Do it even if the birth looked perfect.

- Check every 15 minutes for the first hour, then as ordered for the second hour:
  - Pulse and blood pressure.
  - Uterus: hard, midline, at or just below the umbilicus.
  - Bleeding on the pad and under the buttocks.
  - Bladder: not full. A uterus pushed to one side often means a full bladder.
- Help her drink if she is awake and not vomiting.
- Skin-to-skin and breastfeeding within the first hour if both are well. The baby's first feed helps the uterus contract.
- Check the baby: colour, breathing, temperature, cord clamp, and that identification bands match the mother. Two bands are common practice.
- Perineal comfort. A cold pack may be used if the hospital allows it, wrapped in cloth, not direct ice on skin.
- Let her pass urine. Go with her the first time. She can faint from a low blood volume.
- Do not discharge from the labour room until bleeding is settled, the uterus is hard, and vital signs are stable.
- Teach her to call if she feels a gush, feels faint, or has severe headache or visual change. Preeclampsia can appear after birth too.

**Counter questions**

**79.1 The fundus is high and soft. What do you do?**  
Massage until firm. Express clots if you are trained and the uterus is supported. Give or increase oxytocin as ordered. Check the pad. Call if it does not stay firm.

**79.2 The fundus is high and pushed to one side, and she has not passed urine. What do you do?**  
Help her pass urine. If she cannot, catheterise with aseptic technique. Recheck the fundus. It often becomes firm and central after the bladder is empty. If bleeding was heavy, still inform the doctor.

**79.3 The fundus is firm but she feels dizzy and the pulse is 120. What do you do?**  
Do not be reassured by a firm fundus alone. Look for hidden bleeding, a tear, or bleeding into a hematoma. Lay her flat, oxygen, call the doctor, open a vein line, and check the pad and the perineum. A cervical tear bleeds with a hard uterus.

### 80. What are Leopold's manoeuvres?

**Answer.**  
Leopold's manoeuvres are four abdominal examinations to find how the baby is lying. The mother lies on her back with knees slightly bent, and you warm your hands. Do not do them during a contraction.

1. **Fundal grip.** Face her head. Feel the top of the uterus. A head feels hard, round, and can be moved. A breech feels softer and larger.
2. **Lateral grip.** Feel down both sides. The back is smooth and firm. The limbs feel like small knobs.
3. **First pelvic grip, also called Pawlik's grip.** Face her head. Grasp the lower part just above the pubic bone with one hand. This tells you what is presenting, and whether it moves.
4. **Second pelvic grip.** Turn to face her feet. Slide both hands toward the pelvis. This confirms the presenting part and whether it has entered the pelvis.

A head that is not the presenting part needs a doctor. Do not rupture the membranes of a breech or a high head on your own.

**Counter questions**

**80.1 What lie is normal?**  
A longitudinal lie, with the head down and the back to the mother's front-left, is the common normal position. It is often called left occipito-anterior. Occiput means the back of the baby's head. Anterior means toward the mother's front.

**80.2 She is 32 weeks and the breech is at the bottom. Is that always an emergency?**  
Not by itself at 32 weeks. Many babies turn later. Tell the doctor, and teach her to report bleeding, waters breaking, or reduced movements. Do not frighten her by saying the baby must be delivered that day.

### 81. What are the signs of fetal distress, and what do you do?

**Answer.**  
Fetal distress means the baby is short of oxygen. Signs:

- A fetal heart that stays below 110 or above 160 beats per minute.
- A heart rate that falls late in the contraction and is slow to recover.
- Thick meconium with an abnormal heart rate.
- A sudden stop in movements.

What you do:

1. Call the doctor.
2. Turn the mother to the left side.
3. Stop the oxytocin infusion if it is running.
4. Check the blood pressure. A low pressure from lying flat can slow the fetal heart. Left side corrects that.
5. Give oxygen if ordered, or if she is hypoxic.
6. Start or check the vein line as ordered.
7. Do not send her to walk. Prepare for a possible fast delivery if the heart rate does not recover.

**Counter questions**

**81.1 The heart rate drops during a contraction and recovers as soon as the contraction ends. What do you say?**  
An early drop that mirrors the contraction is often head compression. You still record it and keep listening. A late drop, which starts after the peak and recovers slowly, is more dangerous. If you are not sure which one it is, call the doctor. Do not guess and walk away.

### 82. What is the difference between induction and augmentation of labour?

**Answer.**  
Induction means starting labour when it has not started. Reasons include prolonged pregnancy, severe preeclampsia, rupture of membranes without pains, or a baby who must be delivered. Methods include a prostaglandin tablet or gel on the cervix, an oxytocin drip, or artificial rupture of membranes. The doctor decides. The cervix is scored first, often with the Bishop score.

Augmentation means strengthening a labour that has already started, when contractions are too weak. An oxytocin drip is the usual method.

Artificial rupture of membranes means the skilled person breaks the bag of waters with a sterile instrument. Note the time and the colour. Listen to the fetal heart at once, because the cord can come down.

**Counter questions**

**82.1 Can you increase the oxytocin drip because the pains look weak?**  
No. Oxytocin is a high-alert medicine. Increase it only on a written order, at the ordered interval, while you watch the fetal heart and the rest between contractions. If the uterus does not relax, stop the drip and call.

### 83. What is prolonged labour, and what is obstructed labour?

**Answer.**  
Prolonged labour means progress is too slow. On the partograph, the plot crosses the alert line and may reach the action line. The mother becomes tired, dehydrated, and more likely to bleed or get an infection. The baby becomes distressed.

Obstructed labour means strong contractions cannot push the baby through, because of a small pelvis, a bad position, or a large baby. Warning signs: the head stays high, severe moulding, a huge caput, a tight ring felt in the uterus called Bandl's ring, blood in the urine, maternal exhaustion, and fetal distress. The uterus can rupture.

Nurse's actions: stop oxytocin, call the doctor, left side, oxygen if ordered, a wide vein line, catheter, cross-match, nil by mouth, and prepare theatre. Do not push on the top of the uterus. Fundal pressure can rupture it.

**Counter questions**

**83.1 What is Bandl's ring?**  
It is a late, dangerous groove between the upper and lower uterus in obstructed labour. It means rupture may be near. This is not the normal retraction of a uterus in a healthy second stage. Call the surgeon. Do not wait for the next vaginal examination hour.

### 84. What is the puerperium, and what do you watch?

**Answer.**  
The puerperium is the first 6 weeks after birth, while the mother's body returns toward the non-pregnant state.

- The uterus shrinks about 1 centimetre a day. Just after birth it is near the umbilicus. By about day 10 it is often no longer felt on the abdomen.
- After-pains are cramps, stronger in mothers who have given birth before, and stronger during breastfeeding, because breastfeeding releases oxytocin. Empty the bladder, help her feed, and give the ordered pain relief. Severe pain with heavy bleeding is not a normal after-pain.
- Lochia changes from red, to pink-brown, to white. A foul smell or a sudden heavy bleed needs a doctor.
- Temperature, breast fullness, perineum, legs for swelling and pain, mood, and urine are checked.
- Breast engorgement: feed often, warm the breast before a feed, support it with a firm bra, and use a cold pack after a feed if the hospital allows. Do not stop feeding suddenly.
- Mood: tearfulness on day 3 can be the baby blues. A mother who cannot sleep, will not touch the baby, or talks of harming herself needs an urgent doctor and must not be left alone.

**Counter questions**

**84.1 What is subinvolution?**  
The uterus is not shrinking as expected. The fundus stays high, lochia stays red, and she may bleed or have a fever. Causes include infection and retained pieces. Inform the doctor. Do not call it normal because she is still within 6 weeks.

### 85. What is essential care of the newborn?

**Answer.**  
Essential newborn care is the care every baby needs at birth.

- Dry and warm the baby. Skin-to-skin on the mother's chest if the baby is crying.
- Delay cord clamping for at least 60 seconds if the baby is well.
- Help breastfeeding within the first hour.
- Give vitamin K into the muscle as per protocol, often 1 milligram for a term baby. It prevents bleeding.
- Keep the cord clean and dry. No ash, oil, or cow dung on the cord.
- Put identity bands on the baby and confirm them with the mother.
- Eye care as per hospital protocol.
- Check breathing, colour, temperature, and feeding.

Danger signs to teach the mother: the baby is not feeding, is too hot or too cold, breathes fast, has chest indrawing, has a fit, is yellow on the first day, or has pus or a bad smell at the cord. These need same-day review.

**Counter questions**

**85.1 The family wants to give honey or water before the first milk. What do you say?**  
"The first milk, called colostrum, is the baby's food and protection. Honey and water can harm a new baby. Please put the baby to the breast." Stay and help the latch. Colostrum is the thick yellow milk of the first days. Do not throw it away.

### 86. What are other labour emergencies the examiner may add?

**Answer.**  
Know the first nurse actions. The doctor does the procedure.

**Cord prolapse**  
The cord is felt or seen in the vagina after the waters break, and the baby is not yet born. The cord is compressed, and the baby can die quickly.

- Call for help. Do not leave her.
- Glove and push the presenting part up with two fingers so it does not crush the cord. Do not push the cord back in.
- Position: knee-chest, or head-low with a pillow under the hip, or left lateral with the hips up. The aim is to get the baby off the cord.
- Oxygen to the mother. Stop oxytocin.
- Keep a cord that is outside the vagina moist with a sterile saline gauze. Do not handle it roughly. Cold air can spasm the vessels.
- Continuous fetal heart check.
- The doctor may fill the bladder with saline to hold the head up, and will do an urgent caesarean if birth is not immediate.
- If the cervix is fully dilated and the head is very low, an immediate vaginal birth by a skilled person may be faster. You prepare both paths. You do not argue. You move.

**Meconium-stained liquor with an abnormal fetal heart**  
Call the doctor. Stop oxytocin if it is running. Left lateral position. Oxygen as ordered. Prepare for possible caesarean and for a neonatal team at birth.

**Shoulder dystocia**  
The head is born, but the shoulder is stuck. The chin retracts against the perineum. This is an emergency.

- Call for help. Note the time.
- Do not pull hard on the head. Pulling injures the baby's neck nerves.
- McRoberts manoeuvre is often first: flex the mother's thighs hard onto her abdomen, with helpers. This tilts the pelvis.
- Suprapubic pressure may be applied by a trained person. Suprapubic means just above the pubic bone. The pressure is from the side of the baby's back, downward. This is not fundal pressure. Do not push on the top of the uterus. Fundal pressure makes the shoulder more stuck and can rupture the uterus.
- The doctor may do internal manoeuvres or an episiotomy to make room for the hands.
- After birth, the baby needs a check for arm weakness and a broken collar bone. The mother needs a check for PPH and tears.

**Previous caesarean scar pain in labour**  
Scar rupture warning: sudden pain that then stops, a change in contractions, bleeding, a rising pulse, fainting, and fetal distress. The presenting part may disappear upward. Stop oxytocin. Call the surgeon. Oxygen, vein line, cross-match, and prepare theatre. Do not wait to "see if the pain returns."

**Counter questions**

**86.1 What is the Bishop score?**  
A score of how ready the cervix is for labour. It includes dilatation, effacement, station, how soft the cervix is, and the position of the cervix. A higher score, often 6 or more, means the cervix is favourable for induction. A low score means induction may take longer or may fail. The nurse does not decide induction. You may be asked the name and the purpose.

**86.2 What records do you complete after a birth?**  
Time of birth, sex, Apgar, cord clamp time, placenta complete or not, blood loss, perineal tear or episiotomy, medicines, maternal vital signs, baby identification, and any emergency. Complete the partograph. Register the birth as per hospital policy. Do this before you go off duty.

## 8. Other important viva questions

These are questions a 1 to 3 year staff nurse is often asked, besides the five main topics above.

### 87. What is the difference between cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization?

**Answer.**  
Cleaning removes dirt and most germs with soap and water. It must come before disinfection or sterilization. Disinfection kills many germs, but not all spores. Sterilization kills all germs, including spores.

An autoclave sterilizes with steam under pressure. The setting examiners expect is 121 degrees Celsius, at 15 pounds per square inch, for 15 to 20 minutes. Instruments must be cleaned first, packed so steam can enter, and the pack must show a passed indicator.

Boiling water is not sterilization. It is a lower-level method and does not reliably kill all spores.

**Counter questions**

**87.1 How do you sterilize a heat-sensitive instrument such as a scope?**  
It cannot go in a hot autoclave. The hospital uses a chemical method, often 2 percent glutaraldehyde, for the time written on the protocol. A short soak disinfects. A much longer soak is used when sterilization is required. Rinse with sterile water after, so the chemical does not burn the next patient. Check the expiry of the solution.

**87.2 What is the difference between medical asepsis and surgical asepsis?**  
Medical asepsis reduces germs. Hand hygiene and clean gloves are examples. Surgical asepsis keeps a procedure free of all germs. A sterile dressing, a urinary catheter insertion, and an operation use surgical asepsis. If a sterile item touches a non-sterile surface, it is contaminated. Do not use it.

### 88. What is the chain of infection, and what is a hospital-acquired infection?

**Answer.**  
The chain has six links:

1. The germ.
2. The reservoir, where it lives, such as a person, a wound, or equipment.
3. The way it leaves, such as cough, stool, or blood.
4. The way it travels, such as hands, air, droplets, or a needle.
5. The way it enters the next person, such as a wound, a mouth, or a catheter.
6. A person who can catch it, such as a newborn, a post-operative patient, or a person on steroids.

Break any one link and the infection stops. Hand hygiene breaks the travel link. That is the most useful link a nurse controls.

A hospital-acquired infection, also called a nosocomial infection, is an infection that was not present on admission and appears about 48 hours or more after admission. Examples are a urine infection from a catheter, a wound infection, pneumonia, and a bloodstream infection from a cannula. Prevention is hand hygiene, sterile procedures, removing tubes early, and not giving antibiotics "just in case" without an order.

**Counter questions**

**88.1 What is personal protective equipment?**  
Personal protective equipment, shortened to PPE, is what you wear to block germs. Gloves, gown, mask, and eye protection are chosen for the task. For airborne diseases such as tuberculosis, use an N95 mask, keep the patient in a separate area if possible, and teach cough hygiene. Remove PPE in the safe order your hospital posts, and wash your hands after.

### 89. What antidotes should you remember?

**Answer.**  
An antidote is a medicine that reverses another medicine. These are interview answers. Give them only on a doctor's order.

- Magnesium sulphate: calcium gluconate, 10 millilitres of 10 percent, slow intravenous.
- Opioids such as morphine or pethidine: naloxone.
- Heparin: protamine sulphate.
- Warfarin: vitamin K. Fresh frozen plasma may also be ordered if bleeding is severe.
- Paracetamol overdose: N-acetylcysteine.
- Benzodiazepine overdose, such as diazepam: flumazenil. Use only with a doctor, because it can cause a fit.
- Organophosphorus poisoning, such as some insecticides: atropine, and pralidoxime as ordered. The nurse's first acts are remove contaminated clothes, wash the skin, airway, and oxygen. Do not make a conscious patient vomit unless a poison centre or doctor says so.
- Digoxin toxicity: stop the drug and inform the doctor. A specific antibody is used in severe cases.

**Counter questions**

**89.1 A patient on warfarin has a nosebleed and black stool. What do you do before the antidote arrives?**  
Stop the next warfarin dose. Do not give it while you "wait and see." Call the doctor, check the pulse and blood pressure, keep the patient in bed, and send the clotting test as ordered. Press the soft part of the nose if it is a simple nosebleed, with the patient sitting forward, not lying back swallowing blood.

### 90. How do you calculate an intravenous drip rate?

**Answer.**  
Drops per minute = (volume in millilitres × drop factor) ÷ time in minutes.

The drop factor is written on the set. A common adult set is 20 drops per millilitre. A micro set, used for children and some drug infusions, is 60 drops per millilitre.

Example: 1000 millilitres in 8 hours, drop factor 20.

- 8 hours is 480 minutes.
- 1000 × 20 = 20,000.
- 20,000 ÷ 480 = about 42 drops per minute.

Count for a full minute when you set a high-alert drug. Recheck the rate after the patient changes position. Do not speed a drip to "catch up" potassium, oxytocin, or blood.

**Counter questions**

**90.1 The doctor orders 500 millilitres in 4 hours with a 20-drop set. What is the rate?**  
4 hours is 240 minutes. 500 × 20 = 10,000. 10,000 ÷ 240 = about 42 drops per minute. Say the calculation aloud so the examiner can hear the steps.

### 91. What positions do examiners ask, and when do you use them?

**Answer.**

- **Supine:** flat on the back. Used for examination. Avoid a long time in late pregnancy, because the uterus presses the big vein.
- **Fowler's:** sitting up at about 45 to 60 degrees. High Fowler's is nearer upright. Used for breathlessness.
- **Semi-Fowler's:** about 30 degrees. Comfort after abdominal surgery if ordered.
- **Left lateral:** on the left side. Used in pregnancy, preeclampsia, and fetal distress.
- **Sims':** partly on the left side, with the upper knee bent. Used for enema and some vaginal procedures.
- **Lithotomy:** on the back with legs supported. Used for delivery and perineal repair. Do not leave her in it longer than needed. Watch the legs for numbness.
- **Knee-chest:** on knees and chest. Used in cord prolapse, together with a hand lifting the presenting part.
- **Recovery position:** side-lying when unconscious but breathing.
- **Trendelenburg:** head lower than the feet. It is not routine for shock now. Legs may be raised if she is not breathless. Knee-chest or a pillow under the hip is preferred for cord prolapse if you can keep the pressure off the cord.

**Counter questions**

**91.1 A breathless patient is made to lie flat. What do you do?**  
Sit them up at once, give oxygen as needed, and tell the person who laid them flat why. A flat position can worsen heart failure and asthma. Shock with breathlessness is also not laid flat with the head down.

### 92. How do you care for an unconscious patient?

**Answer.**

1. Airway first. If the person is breathing and the spine is not injured, use the recovery position. Suction if secretions pool.
2. Nothing by mouth. No oral tablets and no water.
3. Side rails up. Bed at a low height. Pressure-area care every 2 hours.
4. Eye care, because the eyes may stay open and dry. Mouth care.
5. Record the Glasgow Coma Scale and the vital signs as ordered.
6. Catheter and nasogastric tube only if ordered.
7. Talk to the patient. Hearing may still be present. Explain what you are doing.
8. Call the doctor for any fall in the score, a seizure, unequal pupils, or a change in breathing.

**Counter questions**

**92.1 The family wants to feed milk by spoon because the patient has not eaten. What do you say?**  
"Milk in the mouth can go into the lungs because he cannot swallow safely. We are giving fluid through the vein. A tube feed will be started only if the doctor orders it, after the tube position is checked." Stay kind. Do not allow the spoon feed.

### 93. How do you pass and care for a Ryle's tube?

**Answer.**  
A Ryle's tube, also called a nasogastric tube, goes from the nose to the stomach. It drains the stomach or is used for feeding.

- Explain, sit the patient up if allowed, and measure from the tip of the nose to the ear lobe, then to the bottom of the breastbone. Mark that length.
- Lubricate the tube. Insert gently through the clearer nostril as the patient swallows, if swallowing is safe.
- Stop at once if the patient coughs hard, goes blue, or cannot speak. The tube may be in the lung.
- Confirm the place before any feed or medicine. Aspirate and check the pH as your protocol says. An X-ray is the sure check if there is any doubt, and it is required in many hospitals before the first feed.
- Do not push a feed if you cannot confirm the place.
- Fix the tube so it does not press the nostril into an ulcer. Flush as ordered. Record the drainage colour and amount.

**Counter questions**

**93.1 The night nurse says the tube is fine because it was used yesterday. The patient has been coughing. What do you do?**  
Do not feed. Coughing can mean the tube has moved. Recheck the position. Inform the doctor if you are unsure. A feed in the lung can kill.

### 94. What do you do for a patient with a high fever?

**Answer.**  
Fever is a body temperature above normal, often 38 degrees Celsius or more. Take the temperature properly, with the correct site, and report it with the pulse and blood pressure.

Nursing care: uncover extra blankets, give fluids if allowed, give the antipyretic medicine that is ordered, and use tepid sponging only if your protocol asks for it. Do not use ice on the skin or alcohol rubs. Collect a blood culture before the antibiotic if that order exists. Do not start an antibiotic early and then try to collect the culture. Watch for rigors, a rash, neck stiffness, low blood pressure, and confusion. Those need the doctor at once.

Types examiners name:

- Continuous: stays high with little change.
- Intermittent: returns to normal between spikes.
- Remittent: falls, but not back to normal.
- Relapsing: fever-free days, then fever again.

**Counter questions**

**94.1 The temperature is 39 degrees and the patient is shivering. Do you sponge now?**  
During a rigor, the patient feels cold and should be covered lightly until the shaking stops. Sponging during the shaking increases distress. After the rigor, recheck the temperature and follow the fever-care order. Inform the doctor about a rigor. It can mean bacteria in the blood.

### 95. What is anaphylaxis, and what dose of adrenaline do you say?

**Answer.**  
Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction. The person may have a rash, swelling of the lips or tongue, wheeze, vomiting, and a fall in blood pressure. It can follow an injection, an antibiotic, or a food.

1. Stop the trigger. Call for help.
2. Lie the person flat, unless breathing is too hard. Then sit them up a little, and lay them flat again if they feel faint.
3. The key medicine is adrenaline into the outer mid-thigh muscle. The adult interview dose is 0.5 millilitres of 1 in 1000 adrenaline, which is 0.5 milligrams. It can be repeated about every 5 minutes if the doctor says the reaction continues.
4. Oxygen, a vein line, and fluids follow. An antihistamine is not the first medicine. Do not delay adrenaline to prepare an antihistamine.

**Counter questions**

**95.1 You gave ampicillin and the patient starts to wheeze and the pulse is weak. What is your first drug sentence to the doctor?**  
"Suspected anaphylaxis after ampicillin. I have stopped the injection. Please order adrenaline 0.5 milligrams of 1 in 1000 into the thigh now." Then stay and prepare it.

### 96. How do you give oral rehydration solution?

**Answer.**  
Oral rehydration solution, shortened to ORS, is a measured mixture of glucose and salts in clean water. It treats dehydration from diarrhoea. Use one full packet in the amount of water written on the packet, usually 1 litre. Do not add extra sugar or mix a half packet into a full litre. Too strong or too weak is harmful.

Give it in small frequent sips. After each loose stool, an adult may need about 200 millilitres more, and a child less, as the chart says. Continue normal feeding. Watch urine, tears, the soft spot in an infant, and the ability to drink.

Do not give ORS by mouth if the person is unconscious, vomiting everything, or in shock. That person needs a vein line. Call the doctor. Blood in the stool, a very sleepy child, or no urine are danger signs.

**Counter questions**

**96.1 The mother mixed the packet in a glass of water because 1 litre looked like too much. What do you do?**  
Discard that mix if it is too concentrated. Explain again and mix a fresh packet in the full amount. A strong mix can worsen diarrhoea, especially in a child.

### 97. What is the national immunization schedule you should be able to say?

**Answer.**  
This is India's Universal Immunization Programme, in simple form. Vaccines and ages can be updated, so say "as per the current national schedule" if a poster in the clinic differs.

- At birth: BCG on the left upper arm into the skin, oral polio vaccine zero dose, and hepatitis B within 24 hours into the outer thigh.
- At 6, 10, and 14 weeks: pentavalent vaccine at all three visits. Pentavalent covers diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b. Oral polio and rotavirus are also given at these visits. Pneumococcal vaccine is usually at 6 weeks and 14 weeks, not at 10 weeks. Fractional inactivated polio vaccine is usually at 6 weeks and 14 weeks, then again at 9 to 12 months.
- At 9 to 12 months: measles-rubella first dose, a pneumococcal booster, vitamin A, and Japanese encephalitis in selected districts.
- At 16 to 24 months: measles-rubella second dose, diphtheria-pertussis-tetanus first booster, oral polio booster, and Japanese encephalitis second dose where it is given. Vitamin A continues every 6 months up to 5 years.
- At 5 to 6 years: diphtheria-pertussis-tetanus second booster.
- At 10 years and 16 years: tetanus-diphtheria vaccine.
- In pregnancy: tetanus-diphtheria. Two doses, 4 weeks apart, starting as early as possible. If she already had two doses in a pregnancy within the last 3 years, one booster is enough. Give it even in labour if she never received it.

BCG means Bacillus Calmette-Guerin, the tuberculosis vaccine. A small scar later is normal. Do not repeat BCG just because you cannot see a scar, unless the protocol says so.

**Counter questions**

**97.1 A child has a mild cold and fever of 37.8 degrees. Do you cancel all vaccines?**  
A mild illness is not a reason to delay. High fever, a serious illness, or a reason that this vaccine must not be given is a reason to wait. Ask the doctor if you are unsure. Never waste a vaccine by drawing it up and then sending the child away without a plan.

**97.2 Where do you give the hepatitis B birth dose, and why not the buttock?**  
Into the outer middle of the thigh muscle. The buttock can place the vaccine into fat, so it works less well, and it risks the sciatic nerve.

### 98. A patient has severe chest pain. What do you do?

**Answer.**  
Think of a heart attack until the doctor says otherwise. The pain may press on the chest, go to the left arm, neck, or jaw, and come with sweat, nausea, and breathlessness.

1. Sit the patient up if they are breathless. Stay with them.
2. Call the doctor and ask for an electrocardiogram.
3. Check the pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation.
4. Give oxygen only if the saturation is low or the patient is breathless. Routine oxygen for every chest pain is no longer advised.
5. Aspirin, often 300 milligrams chewed, is given if the protocol or the doctor allows it, and if there is no allergy and no active bleeding.
6. Do not give a nitrate tablet if the blood pressure is low, or if the patient has taken a tablet for male sexual function in the last day or two. Those combinations can collapse the blood pressure. Ask before you give it.
7. Prepare for transfer to a higher level of care. Do not send the patient walking.

**Counter questions**

**98.1 What does MONA mean, and what is wrong with saying it first?**  
MONA means Morphine, Oxygen, Nitrates, and Aspirin. It is an old memory word. Morphine, oxygen, and nitrates are not automatic. Aspirin and an early electrocardiogram matter. Say what you will actually do: stay, call, vital signs, electrocardiogram, and aspirin if allowed.

### 99. What first aid do you give for a fracture and for a burn?

**Answer.**  
**Fracture.** Do not straighten the limb. Support it as it lies. Check the pulse, colour, and feeling beyond the injury. Cover an open wound with a clean cloth. Do not push bone back in. Give nothing by mouth. Remove rings before the finger swells. Call for help and an X-ray.

**Burn.** Stop the burning. Cool with running tap water for about 20 minutes. Do not use ice, toothpaste, ink, or butter. Do not burst blisters. Cover with a clean cloth. If the face is burned or there is smoke, watch the airway and give oxygen. Remove tight jewellery and clothes that are not stuck. Stuck cloth stays. Estimate the area if asked. In an adult, the rule of 9 is: head 9 percent, each arm 9 percent, each leg 18 percent, front of the trunk 18 percent, back of the trunk 18 percent, and the perineum 1 percent. The patient's own palm is about 1 percent. A child has a larger head, so do not force the adult numbers onto a child. Electrical and chemical burns need the doctor even if the skin mark looks small.

**Counter questions**

**99.1 The hand is pale and the pulse below the fracture is absent. Why is that more urgent than the X-ray?**  
The limb is not getting blood. This can die in a few hours. Tell the doctor at once. Do not wait in the X-ray queue in silence. Keep the limb supported and the patient warm.

**99.2 A chemical spilled on the arm. What do you do?**  
Brush off dry powder first if it is a powder. Then flood with running water for a long time. Do not neutralize acid with alkali yourself. Remove contaminated clothes. Protect yourself with gloves so you are not burned too.

## 9. Scenario questions

For each scenario, say four things: what you see, what you do first, what you tell the doctor, and what you write. Then answer the counter questions.

Questions 100 to 111 are emergencies you may meet in any ward, including collapse, bleeding after birth, a fit in pregnancy, a strangulated hernia, and a medicine error. From question 112, the cases are grouped into general ward emergencies and female ward emergencies.

### 100. Scenario: A patient on the ward suddenly becomes unresponsive

**What you see.**  
You enter and find Mr Rajan, 58 years old, admitted with chest pain yesterday. He does not answer. His chest is not rising normally. He makes a gasping sound.

**What you do first.**

1. Shake and shout. No response.
2. Shout, "Code blue, bed 12. You, bring the crash cart. You, call the doctor."
3. Check pulse and breathing together for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping is not breathing.
4. Lower the bed. Start chest compressions at 100 to 120 per minute, depth 5 to 6 centimetres, in the centre of the chest.
5. When the bag and mask arrive, give 30 compressions and 2 breaths.
6. Attach the defibrillator pads as soon as they arrive. Clear the bed for a shock if advised.
7. When the team arrives, give SBAR: "Mr Rajan, bed 12, admitted for chest pain. I found him unresponsive and gasping two minutes ago. No pulse. CPR is in progress. No known allergy in the file. Last ECG was abnormal." ECG means electrocardiogram.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
The SBAR above, plus last medicines and last vital signs.

**What you write.**  
Time found, condition, start time of CPR, who was called and when, shocks, medicines, response, and the names of the team.

**Counter questions**

**100.1 He has a pulse but he is not breathing well. Do you still do compressions?**  
No. Support breathing with oxygen and a bag-mask. Call the doctor. Recheck the pulse often. If the pulse disappears, start compressions.

**100.2 A visitor is filming. What do you do?**  
Ask them to stop and to step outside. Filming a code breaks privacy. A colleague can escort them. You do not stop compressions to argue.

### 101. Scenario: Heavy bleeding just after a normal birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Lakshmi delivered a baby boy 15 minutes ago. The placenta is out and looks complete. The pad is soaked, there is a clot, and blood is on the sheet. The uterus feels soft. Her pulse is 118. She says she feels dizzy.

**What you do first.**

1. Pull the emergency bell. Call the name of the midwife and the doctor.
2. Massage the uterus until it is firm.
3. Lay her flat, give oxygen, and keep her warm.
4. Open a wide vein line if one is not already running. Start fluids as ordered. Send cross-match.
5. Catheterise the bladder.
6. Give oxytocin as ordered: intramuscular dose if not given, and an infusion. This mother has no high blood pressure in this scenario, but still say the medicine aloud.
7. Weigh or estimate the loss and repeat vital signs.
8. If the uterus becomes hard and bleeding continues, tell the doctor it may be a tear.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Lakshmi has given birth twice before this baby. She delivered 15 minutes ago. The placenta looked complete. She has soaked one pad and has clots. The uterus was soft. Pulse 118. I am massaging, oxygen is on, the bladder is being emptied, and I need you now for an oxytocin infusion and possible theatre. Please order the dose you want."

**What you write.**  
Birth time, time bleeding noticed, estimated loss, uterine tone, medicines with time, urine, vital signs, doctor informed at what time, and the response.

**Counter questions**

**101.1 The doctor is in the operation theatre and cannot come for 10 minutes. What do you do?**  
Do not wait in silence. Continue massage, oxygen, fluids, bladder emptying, and the oxytocin that is already in the standing order or that the duty doctor orders by phone. Call a second doctor or the senior nurse. Keep the mother in the labour room. Recheck pulse. If she becomes cold and drowsy, treat it as shock and escalate again.

**101.2 She has a blood pressure of 150/100. Which drug will you not give?**  
Do not give methylergometrine. Tell the doctor the blood pressure. Oxytocin is the first choice.

### 102. Scenario: A pregnant woman with high blood pressure, headache, and then a seizure

**What you see.**  
Mrs Fatima is 34 weeks pregnant, admitted with blood pressure 170/112 and urine protein 3+. She complains of severe headache and blurred vision. While you are at the bedside, her eyes roll up and her body starts jerking.

**What you do first.**

1. Note the time. Call for help. Call the obstetrician.
2. Turn her to the left side. Protect her head. Do not put a spoon in the mouth.
3. When the jerking stops, open the airway, suction if needed, and give oxygen.
4. Magnesium sulphate loading as ordered, Pritchard regimen, with calcium gluconate ready.
5. Check the fetal heart as soon as the fit is over.
6. Blood pressure as ordered. Do not give methergine later if she bleeds.
7. Prepare for delivery. Keep her nil by mouth.
8. Side rails, suction, and a nurse who stays with her.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Fatima, 34 weeks, known severe preeclampsia. Blood pressure was 170/112, urine protein 3+, headache and blurred vision. She had a fit at 14:10 lasting about one minute. She is on her left side on oxygen. Fetal heart after the fit is ______. Please come now. Magnesium is ready. Do you want the Pritchard loading dose now?"

**What you write.**  
Time of fit, duration, injuries, medicines, blood pressure, urine output, fetal heart, and doctor arrival time.

**Counter questions**

**102.1 Before the fit, she only had a headache. Was that enough to worry?**  
Yes. Headache and blurred vision with this blood pressure mean impending eclampsia. She should already have been on magnesium if the doctor ordered severe-disease protocol, in a quiet bed near the station, with suction ready. Report impending signs early. Do not wait for the fit.

**102.2 Her knee jerk is absent and her breathing is 10 per minute after magnesium. What do you do?**  
Stop magnesium. Call the doctor. Support breathing. Give calcium gluconate 10 millilitres of 10 percent slowly into the vein as ordered. Recheck breathing and the reflex.

**102.3 Her platelets are 40,000. How does that change the injection?**  
That is very low. Intramuscular magnesium can cause a deep bleed. Tell the doctor. The intravenous route is safer. Avoid extra intramuscular injections. Handle her gently.

### 103. Scenario: A boggy uterus on the first day after caesarean birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Anita had a caesarean yesterday for failure to progress. On the morning round, the uterus is soft and above the umbilicus. The pad has a large clot. Pulse is 110. Blood pressure is 100/70. She has not passed much urine.

**What you do first.**  
This is still primary PPH if it is within 24 hours. Caesarean mothers bleed inside as well, so the pad may not show the full loss.

1. Call the doctor now. Massage the uterus.
2. Check the abdominal dressing and the bed for hidden blood. Look at the drain if she has one.
3. Oxygen if she is dizzy or saturating low. Lay her flat.
4. Check the bladder. Catheterise if it is full and she cannot void.
5. Check the intravenous line. Oxytocin infusion may be ordered.
6. Vital signs every 15 minutes. Ask for haemoglobin and cross-match.
7. Do not give methergine until you know her blood pressure history. Her current pressure is not high, but preeclampsia may have been the reason for other medicines. Check the file.
8. Watch for shock. A falling pressure is a late sign. A pulse of 110 is already a warning.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Anita, day 1 after caesarean, uterus boggy and high, large clot on the pad, pulse 110, blood pressure 100/70, poor urine. I am massaging and I need you at the bedside. Please advise oxytocin and whether you want blood tests now."

**What you write.**  
Fundal height and tone, clot size, vital signs, urine, massage, medicines, and the time the doctor was informed.

**Counter questions**

**103.1 The dressing is dry. Does that mean she is not bleeding inside?**  
No. Blood can collect inside the uterus or the abdomen while the skin dressing stays dry. A rising pulse, a large uterus, shoulder pain, a distended abdomen, and dizziness matter. Inform the doctor.

**103.2 She is in pain from the stitches and does not want massage. What do you say?**  
"I know the stitches hurt. Your womb is soft and that can cause heavy bleeding. I will support the lower part and rub gently but firmly. I will ask the doctor for pain relief. I cannot leave the womb soft." Then inform the doctor for analgesia and review.

### 104. Scenario: A hernia patient with sudden severe pain, vomiting, and a tense swelling

**What you see.**  
Mr Yusuf, 50 years old, is admitted for a planned inguinal hernia repair tomorrow. At 2 a.m. he has severe pain in the groin lump. The lump is hard, tender, and will not go back. He vomits twice. His pulse is 104.

**What you do first.**

1. Do not try to push the lump back.
2. Inform the duty doctor and the surgeon at once. This planned case has become an emergency. Strangulation is possible.
3. Nil by mouth.
4. Vital signs, pain score, and a note of vomiting.
5. Vein line and blood tests as ordered. Nasogastric tube if ordered.
6. Pain relief after the doctor has been informed and has ordered it. Tell the doctor the pain is severe so the order is not delayed till morning.
7. Prepare consent and theatre checklist if surgery is decided.
8. Reassure him: "Do not push the swelling. We are calling the surgeon now. You will not eat or drink, because you may need an operation tonight."

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Yusuf, admitted for elective left inguinal hernia, now has a tense tender irreducible lump, vomiting, and pulse 104. I have kept him nil by mouth. I have not attempted reduction. Please see him now for possible strangulation."

**What you write.**  
Time of pain, description of the lump, cough impulse absent, vomiting, pulse, doctor informed, and nil by mouth started.

**Counter questions**

**104.1 He says he always pushes it back himself. Can you help him push once?**  
No. A tender irreducible hernia at night with vomiting is not a simple reducible hernia. Forcing it can push dead bowel inside. The surgeon decides.

**104.2 His pain becomes less and the lump disappears before the doctor arrives. Can you cancel the call?**  
No. Tell the doctor it reduced on its own. Dead bowel can slip back. He still needs examination. Keep him nil by mouth until the doctor says otherwise. Watch pulse, pain, and the abdomen.

### 105. Scenario: The cord is felt in the vagina during labour

**What you see.**  
Mrs Kavya is in labour at 6 centimetres. The waters break with a gush while you are in the room. The fetal heart falls to 80. On examination the cord is felt below the head.

**What you do first.**

1. Do not remove your hand. Push the head up off the cord.
2. Shout for help, the obstetrician, and the theatre team.
3. Explain in one sentence: "I am holding the baby up so the cord is not pressed. Please do not push."
4. Position with help: hips up, or knee-chest, while your hand stays in place.
5. Stop oxytocin. Oxygen. Left side if the position allows, with hips elevated.
6. Continuous fetal heart monitoring.
7. If the cord comes outside, cover it with warm saline gauze. Do not put it back inside.
8. Prepare for immediate caesarean. Keep her nil by mouth. Send blood sample if not already sent. Identity band. Consent is taken by the doctor, urgently.
9. A helper must take over the hand in the vagina if you are exhausted. The pressure must not be released.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Kavya, 6 centimetres, cord prolapse at the time the waters broke. Fetal heart 80. I am elevating the head with my hand. Oxytocin is off. Please come for immediate caesarean."

**What you write.**  
Time of rupture, time cord felt, fetal heart, position used, oxytocin stopped, doctor called, and time of arrival in theatre.

**Counter questions**

**105.1 She wants to go to the toilet first. What do you say?**  
"You cannot sit or walk now. The baby's cord is being pressed. We are going to the operation theatre. I will stay with you." Use a bedpan only if it does not release the cord pressure. Usually there is no time.

**105.2 The fetal heart comes back to 140 while you hold the head. Can you remove your hand?**  
No. The heart is better because you are holding the head off the cord. If you remove your hand, it may drop again. Stay until birth, or until a skilled person takes over.

### 106. Scenario: The baby is not crying at birth

**What you see.**  
A term baby is born. The baby is limp, blue, and not breathing. The cord is clamped because you need to move to the warmer, or a helper clamps it at once so resuscitation is not delayed.

**What you do first.**  
Follow newborn resuscitation steps. Call the paediatrician loudly.

1. Place the baby under a warmer. Dry, remove the wet sheet, and stimulate by rubbing the back. Keep the head in a sniffing or neutral position.
2. If the baby starts to cry and breathe, give routine care: skin-to-skin, warmth, and feeding later.
3. If the baby is not breathing, or the heart rate is below 100: start bag-and-mask ventilation at about 40 to 60 breaths per minute. Watch the chest rise. Use room air to start for a term baby if that is your protocol, and add oxygen if the baby does not improve, as the neonatal team directs.
4. If the heart rate stays below 60 even with good chest rise: start chest compressions with ventilation in the ratio of 3 compressions to 1 breath. Call for more help.
5. The doctor may order adrenaline through the umbilical vein if the heart stays below 60.
6. Do not delay ventilation to do a long suction. Suction the mouth and then the nose only if the airway is blocked by secretions.
7. If there was meconium but the baby is crying and active, give routine care. If the baby is limp, start ventilation. Do not delay breaths for a long attempt at deep tracheal suction. Some older teachers still describe tracheal suction. The safe current action is: help the baby breathe first.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Term baby, born at ______. Limp, not breathing. I dried and stimulated. Bag-and-mask is in progress. Heart rate is ______. Please attend now."

**What you write.**  
Time of birth, condition, time ventilation started, oxygen, heart rate, Apgar, time of cry if it starts, and who attended.

**Counter questions**

**106.1 The family wants you to show them the baby immediately. What do you say?**  
"The baby needs help to breathe. I will bring the baby to you as soon as the breathing is safe. Please stay with the mother." Do not delay ventilation for a photograph or a blessing ritual. A colleague can support the mother.

**106.2 What if the chest does not rise with the bag?**  
Reposition the head to neutral. Check the seal of the mask. Suction only if fluid is blocking. Consider a two-person jaw thrust if you are trained. Call the person skilled in a newborn airway. Do not keep bagging the stomach.

### 107. Scenario: A wrong medicine is almost given, or has been given

**What you see.**  
You are about to give injection methergine 0.2 milligrams to a mother for bleeding. You recheck the file and see she is on treatment for preeclampsia. Her blood pressure is 160/110. You have not injected it yet. In the second part of this scenario, imagine you already injected it.

**What you do first if you have not given it.**

1. Do not give it.
2. Tell the senior nurse and the doctor: "Methergine was ordered on the standard slip, but this mother has preeclampsia with blood pressure 160/110. I have withheld it. Please confirm oxytocin."
3. Give the correct ordered medicine, which is oxytocin, after the order is clear.
4. Fill a near-miss report as per hospital policy. A near-miss is an error that was caught before it reached the patient. Reporting it helps the next nurse. You are not punished in a fair safety system for a good catch. Still report it.

**What you do first if you already gave it.**

1. Stay with the mother. Do not hide it.
2. Tell the doctor at once: "I have given methergine 0.2 milligrams intramuscularly to a preeclampsia mother at 11:05. Her blood pressure now is ______. I am sorry. Please advise."
3. Recheck blood pressure every 5 minutes. Watch for headache, chest pain, and a very high reading.
4. Have the emergency trolley near. The doctor may order a blood pressure medicine.
5. Continue PPH care with oxytocin. Do not give a second dose of methergine.
6. Write the truth in the record: drug, dose, time, reason it was wrong, doctor informed, and the mother's response.
7. Complete the incident form.

**What you write.**  
Facts only. No blame paragraph about a colleague. No alteration of the old order.

**Counter questions**

**107.1 The senior says, "Do not write it. The patient is fine." What do you do?**  
You still inform the doctor and you still record the medicine that was given. A hidden dose confuses the next blood pressure reading. You can say, "I need the doctor to know, because her blood pressure can rise." Patient safety is above a wish to avoid paperwork.

**107.2 How could this be prevented?**  
Check allergy and diagnosis, not only the drug card. High-alert and look-alike medicines are kept apart. Say the reason aloud: "This is methergine. It must not be given in preeclampsia." A second nurse checks uterotonics in a busy labour room if that is the protocol.

### 108. Scenario: Needle-stick injury during a night shift

**What you see.**  
You are recapping a used needle after giving an antibiotic. The needle enters your left index finger. The patient is a general surgery patient. You do not know the HIV status.

**What you do first.**

1. Wash with soap and running water. Do not suck, do not scrub, and do not apply bleach.
2. Tell the night supervisor at once.
3. Identify the source patient. The supervisor arranges source testing as per policy.
4. Go to the emergency or the infection-control pathway the same night. Do not wait until morning. HIV PEP works best within 2 hours and can be started up to 72 hours.
5. Your baseline blood tests are taken.
6. Start the tablets if the doctor advises, for 28 days. A common regimen is tenofovir, lamivudine, and dolutegravir. Take the full course even if you feel nausea. Report side effects.
7. Hepatitis B protection is checked. Vaccine or immunoglobulin may be added.
8. Incident form. Counselling. Follow-up blood tests at the times the infection-control team gives you.
9. You may complete the shift only if you are not dizzy and the supervisor agrees. The first doses should not wait until the shift ends.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Needle-stick at 02:20 from a used hollow needle after an intramuscular injection. Source is bed 7, Mr ______. Wound washed. I need risk assessment and PEP now."

**What you write.**  
Incident form: time, depth, type of needle, visible blood or not, source identity, first aid, and time PEP was offered.

**Counter questions**

**108.1 Should you test the source patient without telling anyone?**  
No. Follow hospital consent and confidentiality policy. The supervisor and the doctor arrange it. You do not draw the patient's blood in secret.

**108.2 What is the lesson from how this injury happened?**  
Recapping caused it. Discard the needle directly into the white sharps container without recapping. Take the container to the bedside before the injection.

### 109. Scenario: Fever, back pain, and rash during a blood transfusion

**What you see.**  
Mr Thomas is receiving the first unit of blood for anaemia after surgery. Ten minutes after the start, he has chills, fever of 38.8 degrees Celsius, low back pain, and a rash. His blood pressure falls from 130/80 to 90/60. His pulse is 120.

**What you do first.**

1. Stop the transfusion. Clamp the blood tubing.
2. Keep the vein open with a new set and normal saline. Do not flush the blood tubing into him.
3. Call the doctor and the blood bank.
4. Recheck the name, hospital number, and blood group on the bag and the wrist band. A mismatch is possible.
5. Oxygen if he is breathless or dizzy. Lay him flat if the pressure is low.
6. Vital signs every 5 minutes.
7. Send the bag, the tubing, and new blood and urine samples as the protocol says. Save the first urine. Dark urine suggests a haemolytic reaction.
8. Do not restart that bag. Do not start the next unit until the doctor and the blood bank clear it.
9. Stay with him. Adrenaline and other medicines only if the doctor orders them for anaphylaxis or shock.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Thomas, bed 3, first unit of packed red cells started at 16:00. At 16:10 he has fever 38.8, back pain, rash, pulse 120, blood pressure 90/60. Transfusion stopped. Saline is running on a new set. Please come now. I am informing the blood bank."

**What you write.**  
Start time, reaction time, volume infused if known, signs, action, doctor and blood bank informed, and samples sent.

**Counter questions**

**109.1 The temperature is up but he only has mild itching and the blood pressure is normal. Is the action different?**  
Stop the transfusion anyway and inform the doctor. Mild itching may be an allergic reaction. The doctor may order an antihistamine and may decide about restarting. You do not decide alone to continue. If there is back pain, dark urine, or low blood pressure, treat it as a serious haemolytic reaction. Never restart that bag.

**109.2 What should have been done in the first 15 minutes?**  
Two-nurse check before the start, baseline vital signs, a slow start, and the nurse staying near the patient for the first 15 minutes. Many serious reactions start then.

### 110. Scenario: A diabetic patient is sweating and confused

**What you see.**  
Mrs Rose, 60 years old, has diabetes. She is on insulin. Before lunch she is sweaty, shaky, and confused. She calls you by the wrong name. Her hands tremble.

**What you do first.**

1. This looks like hypoglycaemia. Check the blood sugar at once with the bedside sugar machine, often called a glucometer. Do not send her alone to the lab.
2. If she can sit and swallow safely: give 15 grams of glucose, such as glucose powder in water, or the hospital glucose preparation. Stay with her.
3. Recheck the sugar in 15 minutes. If it is still below 70 milligrams per decilitre, repeat the glucose.
4. When it is normal, give a small snack or her lunch, so it does not fall again.
5. Inform the doctor. Ask about the next insulin dose. Do not give the scheduled insulin until the doctor advises, if the sugar was low.
6. If she becomes unconscious or cannot swallow: do not pour glucose water into the mouth. Place her on the side. Call for help. Give intravenous dextrose as ordered. Recheck the sugar.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Rose, insulin-dependent, before lunch became sweaty and confused. Sugar was ______. I have given oral glucose. Repeat sugar is ______. Please advise on the lunch insulin."

**What you write.**  
Time, symptoms, sugar values, treatment, response, and doctor informed.

**Counter questions**

**110.1 She is due for insulin and her sugar is 62. The relative says, "Give the insulin, food is coming." What do you do?**  
Do not give the insulin. Treat the low sugar first. Tell the relative, "The sugar is too low. Insulin now can make her unconscious. I am giving glucose and calling the doctor."

**110.2 How do you prevent this on the next shift?**  
Give insulin with the meal, not long before, unless the order is for a specific timing. Tell the patient to report sweating and trembling. Check sugar before insulin. Make sure the meal tray actually arrives. If the patient is nil by mouth for a test, ask the doctor about the insulin dose. Do not give full insulin and then send her fasting unless that order is explicit.

### 111. Scenario: An angry relative at the bedside

**What you see.**  
It is visiting hour. Mr Khan's son is shouting that his father has not been given water, that the nurses are careless, and that he will complain to the police. Other patients are frightened. Mr Khan is post-operative and is actually nil by mouth, with a nasogastric tube.

**What you do first.**

1. Do not shout back. Lower your own voice.
2. Keep a safe distance. Do not block yourself into a corner. Ask a colleague to stay nearby.
3. Move the talk a few steps away from the other patients if it is safe: "I want to understand you. Please come here so I can explain."
4. Listen for one minute without interrupting.
5. Give the fact in simple words: "Your father cannot drink yet because the bowel is resting after surgery. The tube is removing fluid so he does not vomit. We are giving fluid through the vein. I can show you the drip. The doctor plans to start sips when the bowel sounds return."
6. Offer a real action: a doctor update time, a wet mouth swab if allowed, or a chair for the son.
7. If he threatens violence, stop the argument. Call security and the supervisor. Protect the patient and the staff. Do not grab him.
8. Document the concern and what you explained.

**What you tell the doctor or the supervisor.**  
"Mr Khan's son is very angry about nil by mouth. I have explained the reason. He is still agitated and wants a doctor. There is no physical violence yet. Please speak with him."

**What you write.**  
Time, what the relative said, what you explained, who you informed, and the patient's condition. Do not write insulting words about the son. You may quote a short threat if there was one.

**Counter questions**

**111.1 He abuses you personally. What do you say?**  
"I want to help your father. I will not continue if I am abused. Please speak about his care." Then call the supervisor. You do not have to accept abuse. You also do not punish the patient by delaying care.

**111.2 The relative is partly right. The water jug was empty and the patient was allowed sips. What do you do?**  
Apologise for the delay in the sip that was allowed. Give the sip if it is still permitted. Correct the care. Do not invent an excuse. A short honest apology reduces anger: "You are right. The sips were allowed and the jug was empty. I am giving it now. I am sorry for the delay."

**111.3 He asks you to remove the tube because the patient is uncomfortable. What do you do?**  
"I cannot remove it without the doctor's order. Removing it now can cause vomiting and a serious problem. I will call the doctor to review the discomfort and the pain medicine." Then do call.

### General ward emergencies

These are the emergencies a nurse meets on a medical or surgical ward. Stay with the patient, call for help, and use airway, breathing, and circulation first.

### 112. Scenario: Severe chest pain on the general ward

**What you see.**  
Mr Das, 54 years old, is admitted with high blood pressure. He presses his chest and says the pain is heavy. Sweat is on his face. The pain goes to his left arm. His pulse is 110. His oxygen saturation is 96 percent.

**What you do first.**

1. Stay with him. Sit him up if he is breathless. Do not send him walking to the electrocardiogram room.
2. Call the doctor now. Ask for an electrocardiogram at the bedside.
3. Check pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation.
4. Give oxygen only if the saturation is low or he is breathless. His saturation is 96 percent, so do not put oxygen on just because there is pain.
5. If your protocol allows, give aspirin 300 milligrams to chew, after you check allergy and that he is not bleeding. Do not give a nitrate tablet if the blood pressure is low, or if he has taken a tablet for male sexual function in the last day or two.
6. Keep him nil by mouth until the doctor says he will not need an urgent procedure.
7. Attach the monitor if you have one. Bring the crash cart near, but do not start chest compressions while he is awake and has a pulse.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Das, bed 6, sudden heavy chest pain going to the left arm, sweating, pulse 110, blood pressure ______, saturation 96 percent. He is sitting up. Electrocardiogram is being done. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Time the pain started, site, spread, vital signs, what you gave, and the time the doctor was called.

**Counter questions**

**112.1 His blood pressure is 80/50 and he is cold. What changes?**  
Lay him flat if he is not breathless. Oxygen now. Call for help as a collapse risk. Do not give nitrates. Open a vein line as ordered. This may be shock from a heart attack. Stay until the team arrives.

**112.2 The pain goes away after 5 minutes. Can you cancel the call?**  
No. Tell the doctor it eased. He still needs the electrocardiogram. A short pain can still be a heart attack. Keep him in bed.

### 113. Scenario: Sudden breathlessness

**What you see.**  
Mrs Iyer, 60 years old, with a history of asthma, sits up suddenly at 1 a.m. She can speak only one word at a time. You hear a wheeze. Her saturation is 88 percent. She is using her neck muscles to breathe.

**What you do first.**

1. Sit her upright. Do not lay her flat.
2. Call the doctor. Start oxygen to bring the saturation toward 94 to 98 percent.
3. Give the ordered nebulizer, often salbutamol, with oxygen if that is your protocol. Stay while it runs.
4. Check pulse, blood pressure, and the respiratory rate. A silent chest, falling saturation, or exhaustion means she may stop breathing soon. Call again and bring the crash cart near.
5. Ask about a new drug, a smoke exposure, or a missed inhaler. Do not leave her to look for the file for a long time.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Iyer, known asthma, sudden severe wheeze, speaking one word at a time, saturation was 88 percent, now on oxygen and the ordered nebulizer. Please review now."

**What you write.**  
Time, saturation before and after oxygen, respiratory rate, medicine given, and response.

**Counter questions**

**113.1 She has heart failure, not asthma, and pink froth is at her mouth. What is different?**  
Still sit her up, with the legs down if she can tolerate it. Oxygen. Stop any running intravenous fluid. Call the doctor for fluid in the lungs. Do not give a large fast fluid bolus. A diuretic may be ordered. Do not lay her flat for a blood pressure check.

**113.2 The wheeze stops and the chest is quiet, but she is tiring. Is she better?**  
Not always. A silent chest can mean almost no air is moving. This is worse. Call the doctor again and prepare to support her breathing.

### 114. Scenario: Sudden weakness on one side

**What you see.**  
Mr Joseph, 67 years old, was talking at breakfast. His smile is uneven. His right arm falls when he tries to hold it up. His words are slurred. This started 20 minutes ago, and his wife saw the start.

**What you do first.**

1. Note the exact time the symptoms started. That time decides whether a stroke treatment can be given.
2. Call the doctor at once. Do not wait for the round.
3. Keep him nil by mouth. He may choke.
4. Check blood sugar. Low sugar can look like a stroke.
5. Check vital signs and saturation. Give oxygen only if the saturation is low.
6. Lay him with the weak side supported and the airway safe. If he vomits, turn him to the side.
7. Do not give aspirin on your own. A stroke can be a bleed. Aspirin waits until a scan has ruled out bleeding, unless the doctor orders it.
8. Do not send him alone for the scan.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Joseph, sudden right-face and right-arm weakness and slurred speech. Onset 20 minutes ago, witnessed. Sugar is ______. He is nil by mouth. Please see him now for stroke pathway."

**What you write.**  
Onset time, which side is weak, sugar, vital signs, nil by mouth, and doctor called.

**Counter questions**

**114.1 His blood pressure is 190/110. Do you give a blood pressure tablet at once?**  
Do not give it on your own. A sudden drop can worsen a stroke. Tell the doctor the reading. The doctor decides.

**114.2 He becomes more drowsy. What do you do?**  
Recheck the airway and the Glasgow Coma Scale. Call the doctor again. Raise the side rails. Nil by mouth continues. Prepare for urgent transfer.

### 115. Scenario: A fit on the general ward

**What you see.**  
Mr Ali, admitted with a known seizure disorder, starts jerking in bed. His eyes roll up. The fit has just started.

**What you do first.**

1. Note the time. Call for help.
2. Turn him on his side. Protect the head. Do not put a spoon, gag, or finger in the mouth.
3. Move hard objects away. Do not hold his arms and legs down tightly.
4. After the jerking, open the airway, suction if needed, and give oxygen.
5. Check the blood sugar. Stay until he is fully awake.
6. If the fit lasts more than 5 minutes, or fits follow one another without waking, this is status epilepticus. Call the doctor as an emergency. A benzodiazepine may be ordered. Do not give it by mouth during the fit.
7. Look for injury and for a missed dose of his seizure medicine.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Ali, known seizures, fit started at ______. It is still going / it stopped after ______ minute. Sugar is ______. He is on his side on oxygen. Please advise."

**What you write.**  
Start and end time, what the limbs did, sugar, medicines, injuries, and when he woke.

**Counter questions**

**115.1 The patient is a pregnant woman at 36 weeks. Does your action change?**  
Yes. Treat it as eclampsia until the doctor proves another cause. Call the obstetrician as well. Left side, oxygen, magnesium sulphate as ordered, and check the fetal heart after the fit. Do not give only the old epilepsy tablet and walk away.

**115.2 He bit his tongue and is bleeding a little. What do you do?**  
After the fit, turn him so blood drains out, suction gently if needed, and tell the doctor. Do not pack the mouth during the jerking.

### 116. Scenario: Vomiting blood

**What you see.**  
Mr Menon, admitted with liver disease, vomits a large amount of dark red blood. He is pale, pulse 124, blood pressure 90/60.

**What you do first.**

1. Call for help. Turn him on his side so he does not breathe the blood in.
2. Nothing by mouth.
3. Oxygen if he is dizzy or saturating low.
4. Two wide vein lines if you are trained. Send cross-match, haemoglobin, and clotting as ordered. Start fluids as ordered.
5. Save the vomitus so the loss can be seen. Do not throw it away before a nurse has seen the amount.
6. Keep him warm. Repeat vital signs every 15 minutes. A catheter may be ordered to watch urine.
7. Do not give him water, food, or oral medicines.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Menon, known liver disease, large vomit of dark red blood, pulse 124, blood pressure 90/60. He is on his side, nil by mouth. Please come now. I need orders for fluids and cross-match."

**What you write.**  
Time, colour and approximate amount, vital signs, fluids, and doctor informed.

**Counter questions**

**116.1 He passes black stool but is not vomiting. Is that still urgent?**  
Yes. Black tarry stool can be digested blood. Check the pulse. A fast pulse or dizziness needs the same call. Do not wait for visible vomit.

**116.2 Can you pass a nasogastric tube to see if the bleeding has stopped?**  
Only if the doctor orders it. In liver disease there may be swollen veins in the food pipe. A tube put in roughly can tear them. Do not insert it on your own.

### 117. Scenario: High fever with low blood pressure

**What you see.**  
Mrs Rao, day 4 after abdominal surgery, has a temperature of 39.2 degrees Celsius. She is confused. Her pulse is 130. Her blood pressure is 85/50. Her breathing is 28 per minute. The wound is red.

**What you do first.**

1. Treat this as sepsis, which means infection in the blood, until proved otherwise. Call the doctor now.
2. Airway and oxygen if the saturation is low. Lay her flat if she is not breathless. Raise the legs only if she is not breathless.
3. Wide vein line and fluids as ordered. Do not give a huge uncontrolled bolus if she is breathless.
4. Blood cultures before the antibiotic if that can be done at once. Do not delay the antibiotic for a long hunt for bottles. The doctor orders the antibiotic. Give it as soon as it is ordered.
5. Check sugar, urine output, and the wound. Note the line and catheter sites.
6. Repeat vital signs often. Stay with her.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Rao, day 4 post-operative, temperature 39.2, confused, pulse 130, blood pressure 85/50, breathing 28. Wound looks red. I suspect sepsis. Please come now for fluids and antibiotics."

**What you write.**  
Time, vital signs, mental state, cultures sent, antibiotic time, fluid given, and urine.

**Counter questions**

**117.1 The relative asks you to wait for the consultant who comes in the evening. What do you say?**  
"This cannot wait till evening. Low blood pressure with fever can be fatal within hours. I am calling the duty doctor now." Then call.

**117.2 She has a rigor. Do you sponge her during the shaking?**  
Cover her lightly during the shaking. Sponging during a rigor makes it worse. After it stops, recheck the temperature. The low blood pressure is still the priority.

### 118. Scenario: A patient falls from the bed

**What you see.**  
You hear a noise. Mr George, 80 years old, is on the floor beside the bed. He is awake but confused. There is a small cut on his forehead. He says his left hip hurts. He is on blood-thinning medicine.

**What you do first.**

1. Do not lift him alone, and do not pull him up by the arms. Call for help.
2. Check airway, breathing, pulse, and sugar. Look for bleeding from the cut.
3. If he has neck pain, or he is not fully awake, keep the neck in line and log-roll him only with enough people.
4. Check movement, feeling, and pulse in the painful leg. A shortened turned-out leg can mean a hip fracture. Support it as it lies.
5. Inform the doctor. He needs a review before he is put back as if nothing happened, especially because he is on a blood thinner and hit his head.
6. After he is safe, look at why he fell: low blood pressure, a wet floor, a low side rail, a new sleeping tablet, or an urgent need for the toilet.
7. Tell the family. Fill the incident form. Do not hide a fall.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr George fell from the bed. He is awake, has a forehead cut, and left hip pain. He is on a blood thinner. Sugar and vital signs are ______. We have not lifted him alone. Please review before we move him."

**What you write.**  
Time found, position, vital signs, injuries, witnesses, doctor informed, and the incident report number.

**Counter questions**

**118.1 He says he is fine and wants to walk back to bed. What do you do?**  
Do not let him walk. A hip can be broken and a head injury can worsen later. Say, "I need the doctor to check you before you stand."

**118.2 One hour later he vomits and becomes drowsy. What do you do?**  
Call the doctor again at once. Recheck the Glasgow Coma Scale. This can be bleeding inside the head. Nil by mouth. Do not give a sedative to "settle" him.

### 119. Scenario: Rash and collapse after an injection

**What you see.**  
You gave intravenous ampicillin 2 minutes ago. The patient develops a rash, swollen lips, noisy breathing, and a weak pulse. The blood pressure is falling.

**What you do first.**

1. Stop the antibiotic. Do not flush the remaining drug into the vein.
2. Call for help. Lie the patient flat unless breathing is too hard.
3. The first medicine is adrenaline into the outer mid-thigh muscle. The adult interview dose is 0.5 millilitres of 1 in 1000, which is 0.5 milligrams. Give it when the doctor orders it, or under your anaphylaxis protocol. It may be repeated about every 5 minutes if the reaction continues.
4. Oxygen. A new vein line with fluids may be ordered. Do not delay adrenaline in order to prepare an antihistamine.
5. Keep the used syringe and the ampoule for the record. Mark the allergy on the file and the wrist band once the patient is safe.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Anaphylaxis after ampicillin, started 2 minutes ago. Rash, lip swelling, noisy breathing, blood pressure falling. Antibiotic stopped. Please order adrenaline 0.5 milligrams of 1 in 1000 into the thigh now."

**What you write.**  
Drug, dose, time given, time the reaction started, vital signs, adrenaline time, and who was called.

**Counter questions**

**119.1 The blood pressure is normal and there is only mild itching. What do you do?**  
Stop the drug and call the doctor anyway. Mild itching can become anaphylaxis. Do not restart that dose. Watch the airway.

**119.2 Where must adrenaline not be delayed?**  
Do not wait to move the patient to the intensive care unit before the first muscle dose. Give the muscle dose on the ward as ordered, then transfer with oxygen and a nurse.

### 120. Scenario: Sudden breathlessness after surgery

**What you see.**  
Mr Krishnan, day 5 after a fracture surgery, calls you. He was walking less because of pain. He is suddenly breathless and frightened. He has sharp chest pain. Pulse 120. Saturation 90 percent. One calf has been swollen since yesterday.

**What you do first.**

1. Sit him up. Oxygen. Call the doctor now.
2. Do not massage the swollen calf. A clot may be in the lung. This is a pulmonary embolism, which means a blood clot has travelled to the lung.
3. Check blood pressure. If he is cold and the pressure is low, lay him flat only if he can breathe, and treat as shock.
4. Do not send him walking to the X-ray room.
5. Ask for help to bring the monitor and the crash cart near.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Krishnan, day 5 after fracture surgery, sudden breathlessness and chest pain, pulse 120, saturation 90 percent, one swollen calf. I have not massaged the leg. Oxygen is on. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Onset, vital signs, which calf, oxygen, and that massage was not done.

**Counter questions**

**120.1 How could this have been reduced before today?**  
Pain relief so he could move the ankles, early walking as ordered, fluids if allowed, and reporting a swollen calf yesterday. Tell the doctor about a swollen calf the same day. Do not wait for breathlessness.

**120.2 He coughs a little blood. Does that change the plan?**  
It supports a clot in the lung. The same emergency call continues. Save a tissue if he coughs blood, and note the amount.

### 121. Scenario: Very high blood sugar with deep breathing

**What you see.**  
A young man with type 1 diabetes is drowsy. His breathing is deep and fast. His breath smells fruity. The bedside sugar reads "high," above the meter limit. His pulse is 120. The tongue is dry.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor. This can be diabetic ketoacidosis, which means dangerous lack of insulin with acid in the blood.
2. Nothing by mouth if he is drowsy or vomiting.
3. Do not give insulin on your own unless there is a clear order for this situation. The doctor usually orders fluids first and a careful insulin infusion. Potassium can fall and stop the heart, so insulin is not a casual extra dose.
4. Check urine output, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Send the tests the doctor orders.
5. If he becomes unresponsive, check the airway and recheck the sugar. Do not assume every drowsy diabetic has low sugar. This picture is high sugar.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Known type 1 diabetes, drowsy, deep breathing, fruity breath, sugar too high for the meter, pulse 120, dry tongue. Please see him now. I have kept him nil by mouth."

**What you write.**  
Sugar reading, breathing pattern, conscious level, fluids, insulin only if given, and urine.

**Counter questions**

**121.1 A student is about to give him a glass of glucose water. What do you say?**  
"Stop. His sugar is already very high. Glucose water is for low sugar. This patient needs the doctor and fluids through a vein."

**121.2 He has a potassium of 5.5 on the first test. Can the nurse relax about potassium?**  
No. The first potassium can look normal or high, then fall fast once insulin starts. Tell the doctor every new result. Do not add potassium to a drip unless it is ordered, and never give potassium as a fast push.

### 122. Scenario: Severe lower abdominal pain and no urine

**What you see.**  
Mr Paul, 8 hours after hernia repair, has not passed urine. He is restless. The lower abdomen is swollen and dull to percussion. He has the urge but cannot pass urine. The bladder scan, if you have one, shows a full bladder.

**What you do first.**

1. Help him to stand or sit if his operation and blood pressure allow it. Give privacy and running water.
2. If he still cannot pass urine, tell the doctor. A full bladder is painful and can harm the bladder.
3. Catheterise with aseptic technique when it is ordered. Record the volume. A sudden huge volume means the bladder was over-full. Watch for bleeding after decompression and tell the doctor if urine is bloody.
4. Check the wound. Pain from a tight wound is different from a full bladder, but both can exist.
5. Measure later urine. Do not remove the catheter without an order.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Paul, 8 hours after hernia repair, painful full bladder, unable to void. Please advise catheterisation."

**What you write.**  
Hours since surgery, attempts to void, volume drained, colour, and the order.

**Counter questions**

**122.1 Can you press hard on the bladder to force the urine out?**  
No. Hard pressure can injure a full bladder. Use privacy, position, and then a catheter if ordered.

**122.2 This is a woman 6 hours after a normal birth, with the same full bladder. What extra risk is there?**  
A full bladder stops the uterus contracting and can cause heavy bleeding. Empty the bladder and then recheck the fundus. If the uterus is soft, start postpartum haemorrhage care and call the doctor.

### 123. Scenario: The abdominal wound opens

**What you see.**  
On day 7 after a laparotomy, Mr Sen coughs. He feels a gush. The skin stitches have opened. You can see pink bowel on the abdomen.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the surgeon now. Ask someone to bring a sterile pack and warm saline.
2. Do not push the bowel back in. Do not pour antiseptic or powder on it.
3. Cover the bowel with sterile gauze soaked in warm saline. A dry gauze sticks and damages bowel. Keep the dressing moist.
4. Lay him down with the knees slightly bent so the abdomen is not tight. Nothing by mouth.
5. Check pulse and blood pressure. Open a vein line as ordered. Prepare for theatre.
6. Tell him what you are doing, in one calm sentence. Cover him so he is not exposed to the whole ward.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Sen, day 7, wound has opened and bowel is visible after a cough. I have covered it with saline gauze. I have not pushed it back. He is nil by mouth. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Time, what is visible, dressing used, vital signs, and that the bowel was not reduced by the nurse.

**Counter questions**

**123.1 Only a little clear fluid is leaking and no bowel is seen. Is it still urgent?**  
Yes. Tell the surgeon the same day. Fluid from a wound can be the first sign that the deeper layer has opened. Cover with a sterile dressing. Do not pack the wound yourself.

**123.2 He wants to sit up and look. What do you do?**  
Ask him to stay lying with the knees bent. Sitting up can push more bowel out. Hold his hand and explain.

### 124. Scenario: A patient chokes while eating

**What you see.**  
Mr Iqbal is eating lunch. He stands up, holds his throat, and makes no sound. He cannot cough.

**What you do first.**

1. Ask, "Are you choking?" If he cannot speak, this is a severe block.
2. Call for help.
3. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades. If the food is still stuck, give 5 abdominal thrusts above the navel. Repeat 5 and 5.
4. If he becomes unconscious, lower him, call the code, and start cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Look in the mouth before breaths. Remove food only if you can see it. No blind finger sweep.
5. If he is coughing loudly, do not start thrusts. Stay and encourage the cough.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
If the object comes out and he is well, still report it. If he collapsed, report the time, what you did, and whether he is breathing.

**What you write.**  
What he was eating, the manoeuvres, whether the object came out, and his breathing afterwards.

**Counter questions**

**124.1 He is a pregnant woman on the ward who chokes. What changes?**  
Do not use abdominal thrusts. Use 5 back blows, then 5 chest thrusts. Call for help early.

**124.2 After the food comes out he has a harsh cough and a sore throat. What do you do?**  
Let the doctor see him. A piece may still be in the airway. Do not give more food until he is assessed. Watch his saturation.

### 125. Scenario: A patient has taken many tablets and says she wants to die

**What you see.**  
You find Miss Rita drowsy on the bed. Empty strips of her own prescribed tablets are on the locker. She says, "I want to die. Leave me alone."

**What you do first.**

1. Do not leave her alone. Call another nurse and the doctor.
2. Check airway, breathing, pulse, sugar, and saturation. If she is not breathing, start resuscitation. If she is breathing but very drowsy, place her on her side.
3. Nothing by mouth. Do not make her vomit.
4. Collect the strips and any remaining tablets so the doctor can see the names. Remove other medicines from her reach. Do not put them in the general bin where she can get them again.
5. One nurse stays. A person who wants to die is not safe alone in the toilet or behind a closed curtain.
6. Speak calmly. Do not scold. Say, "I am staying with you. The doctor is coming to help."
7. Tell the senior nurse. Ask for a mental-health review. Inform the family through the proper channel.
8. Search the bed for other harmful objects only as your hospital policy allows, with a witness. Keep her dignity.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Miss Rita is drowsy. Empty strips of her prescribed tablets are here. She says she wants to die. Airway is patent, saturation ______, sugar ______. She is on her side. I have not induced vomiting. Please see her now."

**What you write.**  
Her words in quotes, the time found, the names on the strips if readable, vital signs, who stayed with her, and who was informed. Do not write jokes or blame.

**Counter questions**

**125.1 She becomes fully awake and says she was only joking and wants to go home. What do you do?**  
Do not discharge her and do not leave her alone. Tell the doctor. A joke after an overdose still needs a medical and mental-health review.

**125.2 What must you not do?**  
Do not promise to keep it a secret. Do not argue religion or shame her. Do not leave her to "give her privacy" in a locked bathroom. Do not put anything in her mouth to make her vomit.

### 126. Scenario: You find a patient not breathing, and the file says do not resuscitate

**What you see.**  
Mr Samuel is cold, not breathing, and has no pulse. The file has a valid do-not-resuscitate order, signed as your hospital requires. The daughter is at the bedside crying and says, "Please do everything."

**What you do first.**

1. Confirm the order is the current one, for this patient, and that it is valid. If you have any doubt, start cardiopulmonary resuscitation and let the doctor decide.
2. If the order is clearly valid, do not start chest compressions. Call the doctor to certify death.
3. Speak kindly to the daughter: "There is an order, made with the medical plan, not to press on the chest or shock the heart. I am calling the doctor now. I will stay with you."
4. Give privacy. Remove extra equipment after the doctor has seen him, as per last-offices policy. One pillow, clean sheet, and a respectful position.
5. Do not remove tubes until the doctor has certified death, if your policy needs them left for checking.
6. Inform the senior nurse. Complete the death documentation. Look after the family's questions. Do not give the death certificate yourself if that is the doctor's role.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mr Samuel has no pulse and is not breathing. There is a valid do-not-resuscitate order. The daughter is present and is asking for resuscitation. Please come to certify and to speak with her."

**What you write.**  
Time found, absence of pulse and breathing, that the order was checked, time the doctor was called, and time of certification when the doctor writes it.

**Counter questions**

**126.1 There is no written order, only the son saying his father would not have wanted this. What do you do?**  
Start resuscitation and call the code. A relative's spoken wish, without a valid order, is not enough to withhold resuscitation in an emergency. The doctor can decide when the team is there.

**126.2 The patient still has a pulse but is dying and gasping, and the order says comfort care only. What do you do?**  
Do not start compressions. Keep the airway comfortable, give the ordered medicine for distress, and stay with the family. Gasping near death is not the same as a sudden collapse in a person for full treatment. If you are unsure the order covers this, call the doctor immediately and stay.

### 127. Scenario: A snake bite

**What you see.**  
A man is brought to the ward from the emergency holding area after a snake bite on the right foot one hour ago. He is frightened. The foot is swollen. He tied a tight string above the ankle.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor. Lay him down. Keep him still. Do not let him walk.
2. Remove the tight string, rings, and bangles. A tight tourniquet can kill the limb.
3. Do not cut, suck, burn, or put ice or herbs on the bite. Wash gently with soap and water if that is your protocol. Immobilize the limb with a splint, at the level of the heart.
4. Mark the edge of the swelling and write the time. Mark it again later so spread can be seen.
5. Watch breathing, drooping eyelids, bleeding from the gums, and the 20-minute whole-blood clotting test if the doctor orders it. That test is 2 millilitres of blood in a clean glass tube, left still for 20 minutes. If it has not clotted, tell the doctor.
6. Anti-snake venom is given only by the doctor's order, with resuscitation equipment ready, because it can cause anaphylaxis.
7. Nothing by mouth until the doctor says swallowing is safe. Weakness of the muscles can affect swallowing.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Snake bite on the right foot one hour ago. Swelling marked. Tight string removed. He is still, splinted. Breathing is ______. Please review for anti-snake venom."

**What you write.**  
Time of bite, site, first aid already done by the family, swelling mark, symptoms, and venom time if given.

**Counter questions**

**127.1 The family brings the live snake in a bag. What do you do?**  
Do not open the bag on the ward. Keep people away and tell security or the person your hospital uses. Identifying the snake must not create a second bite. Treatment follows the syndrome and the clotting test, not a guess.

**127.2 He has drooping eyelids and a weak voice. What is the priority?**  
The airway. Call the doctor at once. Prepare suction and a bag-mask. Do not give him water. This can be a neurotoxic bite.

### 128. Scenario: A dog bite

**What you see.**  
A child is brought after a neighbour's dog bit the leg. The skin is torn and bleeding. The dog ran away.

**What you do first.**

1. Wash the wound with soap and running water for about 15 minutes as soon as possible. This is the most important first act against rabies.
2. Do not scrub into the depth in a way that damages tissue, but do clean it well. Do not put chili, lime, or ash on it.
3. Do not stitch a dog bite at once. Tell the doctor. Stitching too early can trap rabies virus.
4. Ask about tetanus immunization.
5. This is a category 3 bite because the teeth broke the skin. The doctor orders rabies vaccine and, for category 3, rabies immunoglobulin into and around the wound. Vaccine is given into the upper arm muscle, not the buttock.
6. Tell the family the vaccine schedule must be completed even if the wound looks better tomorrow. The clinic will give the dates. A common intramuscular schedule taught in interviews is days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28. Follow your national schedule if it differs.
7. Comfort the child. Ask an adult to stay.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Dog bite with broken skin on the leg. Wound washed. Dog not available to watch. Please advise rabies vaccine, immunoglobulin, and tetanus."

**What you write.**  
Time of bite, site, washing done, animal status, injections given, and the next dose date.

**Counter questions**

**128.1 The skin is not broken. The dog only licked intact skin. What category is that?**  
That is usually category 1. Wash the skin. Vaccine is not routinely needed for intact skin. If you are unsure whether the skin broke, ask the doctor to look.

**128.2 The family wants the injection in the buttock because the child fears the arm. What do you say?**  
"The rabies vaccine does not work well from the buttock, and it can injure a nerve. We use the upper arm." Then give it as ordered, with a helper holding the child kindly.

### 129. Scenario: Insecticide poisoning

**What you see.**  
A farmer is brought in smelling of insecticide. He is sweating, vomiting, and has small pupils. There is saliva at his mouth. His clothes are wet with the chemical.

**What you do first.**

1. Protect yourself. Wear gloves. Do not get the chemical on your skin.
2. Call the doctor. Airway first. Suction saliva. Oxygen. Place him on his side if he is vomiting.
3. Remove contaminated clothes and wash the skin with soap and water so absorption stops. Keep the clothes in a bag, away from other patients.
4. Do not make him vomit if he is drowsy. Do not give a home mixture.
5. Atropine and pralidoxime are doctor-ordered antidotes for this kind of poisoning. The nurse watches the chest for secretions and the breathing.
6. A urinary catheter and close nursing are often needed. Do not leave him alone.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Suspected insecticide poisoning. Smell of chemical, small pupils, heavy saliva, vomiting. Clothes removed, skin washed, oxygen on. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Time, name of the chemical if the bottle is known, vital signs, pupil size, oxygen, antidote times, and skin wash.

**Counter questions**

**129.1 The bottle label is missing. Does treatment wait until the name is known?**  
No. Treat the airway and the signs. Tell the doctor the smell, the pupils, and the saliva. The bottle can be identified later. Do not taste it or sniff it closely.

**129.2 He has a seizure. What do you do?**  
The same fit care: side, no object in the mouth, time the fit, oxygen after, and call. Do not put atropine into the mouth.

### Female ward emergencies

These cases are for the antenatal bed, the postnatal bed, and the gynaecology ward. Labour-room births are already in the earlier scenarios. A woman on the female ward can still bleed, fit, or collapse.

### 130. Scenario: Fresh bleeding in late pregnancy

**What you see.**  
Mrs Banu, 34 weeks pregnant, is on the antenatal ward for rest. She calls because her clothes are soaked with fresh red blood. She has some abdominal pain. The fetal heart is 100 beats per minute.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the obstetrician now. Do not send her to the scan room alone.
2. Do not do a vaginal examination. If the placenta is covering the cervix, a finger can cause a fatal bleed. This is antepartum haemorrhage, which means bleeding before birth.
3. Lay her on the left side. Oxygen. Wide vein lines. Send cross-match. Pad count. Nothing by mouth.
4. Check the fetal heart again and the blood pressure. A hard woody uterus and pain suggest the placenta is separating early. Painless bleeding suggests a low placenta. You report both patterns, but you do not delay care to decide the name.
5. Watch urine output and consciousness. Prepare for theatre if the doctor says so.
6. Keep the baby cot and the neonatal team informed if delivery is likely.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Banu, 34 weeks, fresh heavy bleeding, abdominal pain, fetal heart 100. No vaginal examination done. She is on the left side, oxygen on. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Time, amount on the pad, pain or no pain, fetal heart, vital signs, and that no vaginal examination was done.

**Counter questions**

**130.1 A junior doctor on the phone says, "Do a quick internal examination and tell me the dilatation." What do you say?**  
"I will not do a vaginal examination until placenta previa is excluded, because it can cause massive bleeding. Please come to see her." Placenta previa means the placenta is lying over the cervix.

**130.2 The bleeding is only a small dark show and she has regular pains. What do you do?**  
That may be labour, not a heavy haemorrhage. Still tell the doctor, check the fetal heart, and transfer to the labour room with a nurse. Any fresh heavy bleeding is still treated as dangerous.

### 131. Scenario: Collapse with a missed period

**What you see.**  
Miss Farah, 28 years old, is admitted to the female ward with lower abdominal pain. She faints while walking to the toilet. Her pulse is 130. Her blood pressure is 80/50. She has shoulder-tip pain. Her period is 6 weeks late. There is slight spotting.

**What you do first.**

1. Call for help. Lay her flat. Oxygen. Do not send her back to walk.
2. This can be a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, which means a pregnancy growing outside the uterus, often in the tube, that has burst.
3. Two wide vein lines, cross-match, and fluids as ordered. Nothing by mouth.
4. A urine pregnancy test is urgent if it is not already done.
5. Do not do a rough vaginal examination. Prepare for theatre. Abdominal pain, fainting, and a late period in a woman of childbearing age is ectopic until the doctor rules it out.
6. Inform a senior even if the pregnancy test is still pending. Do not wait for the morning ultrasound if she is in shock.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Miss Farah, 6 weeks late, collapse, shoulder-tip pain, spotting, pulse 130, blood pressure 80/50. I suspect a ruptured ectopic. Oxygen is on. Please come now for theatre."

**What you write.**  
Time of collapse, last menstrual period, pain, shoulder pain, vital signs, pregnancy test result, and fluids.

**Counter questions**

**131.1 The pregnancy test is negative, but she is still in shock. What do you do?**  
Stay with the emergency care. A test can be wrong, or another bleed can be the cause. The doctor must still see a shocked woman now. Do not send her home on a negative test alone.

**131.2 Why does the shoulder hurt when the problem is in the pelvis?**  
Blood under the diaphragm irritates a nerve that is felt in the shoulder. Shoulder-tip pain with collapse and a late period is a warning, not a muscle strain. Do not give a pain balm and ignore it.

### 132. Scenario: Heavy bleeding in early pregnancy

**What you see.**  
Mrs Noor, 10 weeks pregnant, is on the female ward with pain and bleeding. She passes a large clot and tissue. She is pale. Pulse 118. She feels dizzy.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor. Save the clot and tissue in a container for the doctor to see. Do not flush it away.
2. Pad count. Oxygen if dizzy. Vein line, cross-match, fluids as ordered. Nothing by mouth.
3. Check the blood pressure and the amount of bleeding every 15 minutes if it is heavy.
4. Do not do a vaginal examination unless you are trained and the doctor has asked you to do it in this situation.
5. If she has fever and foul discharge as well, say so. That can be an infected miscarriage, and delay is dangerous.
6. Give emotional care. She may be grieving. Do not say "it was only a clot" or "you can have another soon."

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Noor, 10 weeks, heavy bleeding, has passed clot and tissue which I have kept, pulse 118, dizzy. Please see her now."

**What you write.**  
Weeks of pregnancy, amount, whether tissue was seen, vital signs, and what was saved.

**Counter questions**

**132.1 She is feverish, tender, and the discharge smells foul. What is the extra danger?**  
Infection in the uterus can become septic shock. Call now, not at the next round. Note the temperature. Do not wait for bleeding to stop before reporting the fever.

**132.2 She asks if the pregnancy can still continue. What do you say?**  
"I cannot promise that. The doctor must examine what has been passed and scan if needed. I will stay with you." Do not guess.

### 133. Scenario: Heavy bleeding on day 6 after birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Leela delivered 6 days ago and is on the postnatal ward. She soaks a pad in 30 minutes and passes a clot. The uterus is a little high and tender. She has a low fever. Pulse 112.

**What you do first.**

1. This is secondary postpartum haemorrhage, which means heavy bleeding from 24 hours after birth up to about 6 to 12 weeks.
2. Call the doctor. Massage the uterus if it is soft. Help her pass urine or catheterise if the bladder is full.
3. Oxygen if she is dizzy. Vein line. Pad weight or count. Cross-match as ordered.
4. Oxytocin may be ordered. Do not give methylergometrine if she has high blood pressure.
5. Do not do a rough vaginal examination on the ward. Retained pieces or infection may need ultrasound and theatre.
6. Antibiotics may be ordered because fever and a tender uterus suggest infection. Give them when ordered.
7. Watch the baby. If the mother goes to theatre, a nurse must plan the baby's feeding.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Leela, day 6 postnatal, soaking a pad in 30 minutes, uterus high and tender, low fever, pulse 112. Please see her now."

**What you write.**  
Pad count, clot, fundal height, temperature, medicines, and baby-care plan.

**Counter questions**

**133.1 The bleeding settles after she passes urine. Can you stop the review?**  
Tell the doctor it reduced, but still ask for a review the same shift. Secondary bleeding can return. Record the urine and the next pad.

**133.2 She is breastfeeding. Do you stop the feeds because she may need theatre?**  
Stop oral intake if theatre is likely. Until then, a feed can help the uterus contract if she is stable. If she must be nil by mouth, help her express later so the milk supply continues. The baby can take expressed milk or formula as the paediatric plan says.

### 134. Scenario: Fever and foul discharge after birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Fatima, day 3 after a long labour, has a temperature of 38.8 degrees Celsius. She has lower abdominal pain. The lochia smells foul. The uterus is tender. Pulse 120.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor now. This is puerperal sepsis until proved otherwise. Puerperal means related to the period after birth.
2. Vital signs, including blood pressure. If the pressure is low, treat it as septic shock: oxygen, fluids as ordered, stay with her.
3. Note the perineum, the caesarean wound if any, the breasts, the urine, and the calves. Fever after birth is not always the uterus. A breast infection, a urine infection, a wound, or a clot in the leg can also cause fever. Report what you find.
4. Lochia amount and smell. Culture swabs only as ordered. Do not do repeated vaginal examinations.
5. Encourage fluids if she is not in shock and not going soon to theatre. Breastfeeding can continue if she is able and the paediatric plan agrees. Help her wash her hands.
6. Watch the baby for fever or poor feeding. Tell the nursery nurse.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Fatima, day 3, temperature 38.8, tender uterus, foul lochia, pulse 120, blood pressure ______. Please review now for puerperal infection."

**What you write.**  
Temperature pattern, lochia, abdominal tenderness, other sites checked, and baby condition.

**Counter questions**

**134.1 The breasts are hard, red, and painful, and the uterus is not tender. What do you think?**  
Think mastitis, which is breast infection, or engorgement. Still report the fever. Help a good latch and emptying of the breast. Do not stop feeding on the red side unless the doctor says so. Antibiotics, if ordered, must be suitable for breastfeeding.

**134.2 She has a painful swollen calf and a mild fever, but the lochia is normal. What do you do?**  
Do not massage the calf. Tell the doctor the same day. This can be a clot in the leg, and it can move to the lung.

### 135. Scenario: Severe perineal pain a few hours after birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Ruth had a normal birth 4 hours ago. She is crying with perineal pain. The stitch area is tense, blue, and swelling. She cannot pass urine. Her pulse is 118. The pad is only moderately stained.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor. Do not say, "Stitches always hurt." A growing painful swelling can be a hematoma, which is a collection of blood.
2. Check the pulse and blood pressure. Hidden blood loss can cause shock even when the pad looks only moderate.
3. Mark the size of the swelling if you can do it gently, or describe it. Do not press it hard.
4. Help her pass urine. If she cannot, tell the doctor. A catheter may be needed.
5. Cold pack wrapped in cloth may be used if your protocol allows. No ice directly on the skin.
6. She may need theatre to evacuate the clot. Keep her nil by mouth once the doctor decides that. Vein line and cross-match as ordered.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Ruth, 4 hours after birth, severe perineal pain, tense blue swelling, cannot pass urine, pulse 118. Pad is not fully soaked. Please see her now for a possible hematoma."

**What you write.**  
Pain score, size and colour of the swelling, urine, vital signs, and doctor called.

**Counter questions**

**135.1 The uterus is firm. Does that rule out bleeding?**  
No. The bleeding is into the perineum or vagina, not only from a soft uterus. A firm fundus does not make a hematoma safe.

**135.2 She feels rectal pressure, as if she must pass stool. What does that suggest?**  
A collection of blood may be pushing on the rectum. Tell the doctor. Do not give a laxative and wait.

### 136. Scenario: A postnatal mother cannot pass urine

**What you see.**  
Mrs Anita delivered 7 hours ago. She has the urge but cannot pass urine. The uterus is high and pushed to one side. The bleeding is heavier than on the last check.

**What you do first.**

1. Help her to the toilet with a nurse, because she can faint. Privacy, running water, and a comfortable position.
2. If she cannot void and the bladder is full, tell the doctor and catheterise with aseptic technique when allowed.
3. After the bladder is empty, recheck the fundus. It should become firmer and central. Massage if it is soft. Call if bleeding continues.
4. Record the volume. Watch the next pads.
5. Look for perineal swelling that may be blocking urine. That needs the doctor too.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Anita, 7 hours postnatal, cannot void, fundus high and to one side, bleeding heavier. I am helping her to void and will catheterise if she cannot."

**What you write.**  
Hours since birth, attempts, volume, fundal height before and after, and bleeding.

**Counter questions**

**136.1 She passes only 50 millilitres and the bladder still feels full. What do you do?**  
That is not an empty bladder. Tell the doctor. She may need a catheter. Recheck the uterus.

**136.2 Why is this an emergency and not just discomfort?**  
A full bladder causes the uterus to relax and bleed. It can also injure the bladder if it is left over-full. Empty it and recheck the bleeding.

### 137. Scenario: The waters break at 33 weeks on the antenatal ward

**What you see.**  
Mrs Celin, 33 weeks pregnant, says she felt a gush in bed. The pad shows clear fluid. She is not in strong pain. The fetal heart is 148.

**What you do first.**

1. Note the time, colour, amount, and smell. Check the fetal heart at once.
2. Call the obstetrician. Do not do a digital vaginal examination. At this early week, extra examinations increase infection. Also rule out a cord before anyone examines her.
3. If you can see or feel the cord, treat it as cord prolapse: hand lifts the presenting part, hips up, urgent theatre. That is scenario 105.
4. If the fluid is clear and the cord is not felt, keep her in bed, left side, sterile pad. Take her temperature.
5. Watch for fever, a tender uterus, and a change in the fetal heart. Green fluid or bleeding is reported at once.
6. Steroids for the baby's lungs and antibiotics are doctor orders. Do not start them yourself. Prepare transfer to the labour ward or a nursery-ready unit if ordered.
7. Tell her not to sit in a bath or put anything in the vagina.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Celin, 33 weeks, clear liquor at ______, fetal heart 148, no cord felt, no strong pains. No digital examination done. Please review."

**What you write.**  
Time of rupture, colour, fetal heart, temperature, and examinations avoided.

**Counter questions**

**137.1 The fluid is green. What do you add?**  
Tell the doctor at once and ask the neonatal team to be ready. Green meconium at 33 weeks is a warning. Keep listening to the fetal heart. Meconium is the baby's first stool passed into the fluid.

**137.2 She wants to walk to the bathroom. What do you say?**  
"Please use a bedpan for now. Walking just after the waters break can let the cord come down. A nurse will stay near."

### 138. Scenario: One painful swollen leg after birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Devi, day 4 after a caesarean, has pain in the left calf. The left calf is swollen, warm, and larger than the right. She has a mild fever. Her chest is clear.

**What you do first.**

1. Tell the doctor the same day. This can be a deep vein thrombosis, which means a clot in a deep vein.
2. Bed rest until the doctor says she may walk. Do not massage the leg. Do not put a tight bandage on it yourself.
3. Measure both calves at the same point and write the numbers. Elevate the leg a little if that is comfortable.
4. Watch for sudden breathlessness or chest pain. That can mean the clot has gone to the lung. Then give oxygen and call as an emergency.
5. Blood-thinning medicine is a doctor order. Check if she is already bleeding too heavily before any injection. Tell the doctor the pad amount.
6. She can still breastfeed unless the doctor says the chosen medicine is unsafe. Ask. Do not stop feeding on your own.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Devi, day 4 after caesarean, left calf swollen and painful, 3 centimetres larger than the right, mild fever, no breathlessness. Please review today for a possible clot."

**What you write.**  
Measurements, colour, pain, chest symptoms, and advice given about not massaging.

**Counter questions**

**138.1 She is suddenly breathless while you are measuring the leg. What do you do?**  
Stop the examination. Sit her up, oxygen, call the obstetrician and the emergency team. Do not send her for a walk or a scan alone. This may be a clot in the lung.

**138.2 Can early walking after caesarean prevent this?**  
Yes, when the surgeon has allowed walking. Ankle movement, pain relief, and avoiding dehydration also help. Report a swollen leg early. Prevention does not replace today's emergency review.

### 139. Scenario: A mother will not sleep and is afraid she may harm the baby

**What you see.**  
Mrs Rosa, day 5 postnatal, has not slept for two nights. She says the baby is in danger and that a voice is telling her to harm the baby. She is agitated. The baby is in the cot beside her.

**What you do first.**

1. Do not leave her alone with the baby. Call another nurse to take the baby to a safe, supervised place. Tell her the baby is safe and she is not being punished.
2. Call the doctor now. This can be postpartum psychosis, which is a severe mental illness after birth. It is an emergency, not "baby blues."
3. Stay with her. Remove medicines, sharp objects, and cords from reach, calmly, with a witness.
4. Speak simply. Do not argue about the voice. Do not shame her.
5. One-to-one observation. She should not go to the toilet alone.
6. The doctor may need a mental-health colleague. Your job is safety of the mother and the baby, and a clear report.
7. Help the baby to feed by another safe plan. Expressed milk if she is well enough later. Do not force her to breastfeed during the crisis.
8. Inform the senior nurse and the family through the doctor, with privacy.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Rosa, day 5, no sleep for two nights, says a voice is telling her to harm the baby. The baby has been moved to supervised care. She is not alone. Please see her now."

**What you write.**  
Her words in quotes, sleep, behaviour, where the baby is, who is staying with her, and who was informed.

**Counter questions**

**139.1 She only cries in the evening and says she feels overwhelmed, but she is caring safely for the baby and has no plan to harm anyone. What do you do?**  
Listen, tell the doctor the same day, and offer rest and a family helper. Mild tearfulness around day 3 can be baby blues. You still report it. You do not separate the baby if she is safe and caring. If hopelessness, no sleep, or any thought of harm appears, treat it as urgent.

**139.2 She asks you not to tell anyone. What do you say?**  
"I cannot keep this a secret, because you and the baby need help tonight. I will tell the doctor and stay with you." Safety comes before secrecy.

### 140. Scenario: Hidden bleeding after caesarean on the postnatal ward

**What you see.**  
Mrs Jane had a caesarean 10 hours ago. The abdominal dressing is dry. She is restless and dizzy. The abdomen looks more distended. Pulse 126. Blood pressure 96/60. Urine in the catheter is only 15 millilitres in the last hour. The vaginal pad has a small clot only.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the surgeon now. A dry dressing does not rule out bleeding inside.
2. Lay her flat. Oxygen. Check the dressing, the bed, and the drain if she has one.
3. Open or speed the vein line only as ordered. Send haemoglobin and cross-match.
4. Feel the uterus gently if you are allowed. Do not press a fresh caesarean wound hard.
5. Repeat pulse, blood pressure, and urine every 15 minutes. A falling urine output and a rising pulse are shock signs.
6. Nothing by mouth. Prepare for a return to theatre. Keep the baby supervised if she is moved.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Jane, 10 hours after caesarean, dressing dry, but pulse 126, blood pressure 96/60, abdomen distended, urine 15 millilitres in one hour. I suspect internal bleeding. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Dressing status, abdominal girth if measured, drain amount, urine, vital signs, and the time the surgeon was called.

**Counter questions**

**140.1 The pad is dry, so the relative says she is fine. What do you say?**  
"The dressing and the pad can stay dry while blood collects inside. Her fast pulse and low urine tell us she needs the surgeon now."

**140.2 She has shoulder pain and the abdomen is distending. What do you add?**  
Tell the surgeon about the shoulder pain. It can happen when blood irritates the diaphragm. Do not treat it as simple gas and give a drink.

### 141. Scenario: Severe headache on day 2 after birth

**What you see.**  
Mrs Khan, day 2 after a normal birth, has a severe headache and blurred vision. Her blood pressure is 172/114. She has not had a fit. Urine protein is 2+. Reflexes are brisk.

**What you do first.**

1. This is severe preeclampsia after birth. She can still fit. Call the obstetrician.
2. Do not leave her alone. Quiet bed, left side, side rails, suction and oxygen ready.
3. Check breathing, reflexes, and urine output. Magnesium sulphate may be ordered. Keep calcium gluconate ready.
4. Do not give methylergometrine if she bleeds later.
5. Blood tests as ordered, especially platelets and liver tests.
6. Dim light and less noise. Report any twitch at once.
7. The baby stays supervised. She should not walk alone to a far toilet.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Khan, day 2 postnatal, headache, blurred vision, blood pressure 172/114, urine protein 2+, brisk reflexes, no fit yet. Please start severe preeclampsia care now."

**What you write.**  
Blood pressure, symptoms, protein, reflexes, medicines, and that she was not left alone.

**Counter questions**

**141.1 She fits while you are waiting for the doctor. What do you do?**  
Time the fit, left side, nothing in the mouth, oxygen after the jerking, call again, and give the magnesium loading dose if a standing order exists. Check injury. Stay.

**141.2 Her blood pressure tablet from home is an angiotensin receptor blocker, a "sartan" tablet. Can she restart it now that the baby is born?**  
Do not restart it yourself. Some of these tablets are unsafe if she is breastfeeding, and the acute high reading needs the obstetric plan. Ask the doctor. If she is not breastfeeding, the physician still chooses the tablet. Your action tonight is the emergency blood pressure and fit prevention.

### 142. Scenario: The baby stops breathing in the mother's room

**What you see.**  
On the postnatal ward, a 6-hour-old baby in the cot beside the mother is blue and limp. The mother shouts. The baby is not breathing.

**What you do first.**

1. Call for help and the paediatrician. Move the baby to the resuscitation cot or a firm surface.
2. Dry and stimulate if not already dry. Open the airway in a neutral position. Suction only if the mouth is blocked.
3. If the baby does not breathe, or the heart rate is below 100, start bag-and-mask breaths at about 40 to 60 per minute. Watch the chest rise.
4. If the heart rate stays below 60 despite good breaths, start compressions with breaths in the ratio of 3 to 1, and keep calling.
5. One nurse stays with the mother. Say, "We are helping the baby breathe. I will not leave the baby."
6. Do not delay breaths for photos, bath, or feeding.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Six-hour-old baby, found blue and not breathing in the cot. Stimulation done. Bag-and-mask is in progress. Heart rate is ______. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Time found, colour, breathing, time breaths started, heart rate, who attended, and the mother's information.

**Counter questions**

**142.1 The baby starts crying and turns pink. What do you do next?**  
Do not put the baby back and walk away. The paediatrician must still review why it happened. Check the sugar if ordered. Keep the baby warm and observed. Tell the mother what happened in simple words.

**142.2 The mother wants to hold the baby during the bag-mask breaths. What do you say?**  
"I need the baby on a firm surface to breathe for him. Please stay where you can see us." A colleague supports her.

### 143. Scenario: A pregnant woman cannot stop vomiting

**What you see.**  
Mrs Hema, 12 weeks pregnant, has vomited all day. She cannot keep water down. She is dizzy when she stands. The tongue is dry. Urine is dark and small in amount. A ward test shows ketones in the urine.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the doctor. This can be hyperemesis gravidarum, which means severe vomiting of pregnancy, not a normal morning nausea.
2. Check pulse, blood pressure lying and standing if she can stand safely, and weight if it is already in the file.
3. Nothing by mouth until the vomiting settles and the doctor allows sips. Intravenous fluids are ordered by the doctor. Do not force coconut water or juice if she is vomiting it back.
4. Watch for blood in the vomit, confusion, or a very fast pulse. Report those at once.
5. A screen for infection and urine infection may be ordered. Give antiemetic medicine only as ordered, and use a medicine that is suitable in pregnancy.
6. Mouth care. A bowl and a nurse nearby. Do not leave her flat on her back while she is vomiting.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Hema, 12 weeks, vomiting all day, unable to retain water, dizzy, dry tongue, ketones in urine. Please see her for intravenous fluids."

**What you write.**  
Intake, vomit, urine, ketones, lying and standing pulse, and fluids given.

**Counter questions**

**143.1 She has pain on one side and spotting as well as vomiting. What do you add?**  
Tell the doctor at once. Do not call it simple morning sickness. Pain, spotting, and dizziness can be an ectopic pregnancy. Check the pulse and keep her in bed.

**143.2 She asks for her home tablet, which she uses for migraine, but you do not know if it is safe in pregnancy. What do you do?**  
Do not give it from her bag. Ask the doctor. Some migraine tablets are unsafe in pregnancy. You can still give the ordered antiemetic and fluids.

### 144. Scenario: Heavy vaginal bleeding after a gynaecology operation

**What you see.**  
Mrs Lalitha, 8 hours after a hysterectomy, which means removal of the uterus, has soaked two vaginal pads in an hour. She is pale. Pulse 122. Blood pressure 88/54. The abdominal dressing is dry.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the gynaecologist now.
2. Lay her flat. Oxygen. Vein line, cross-match, fluids as ordered.
3. Count the pads. Look under the buttocks. Do not do a vaginal examination yourself.
4. Check the abdominal dressing and any drain. A dry abdominal dressing does not cancel vaginal bleeding.
5. Nothing by mouth. Repeat vital signs every 15 minutes. Urine output if she has a catheter.
6. Prepare for a return to theatre. Keep her warm.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Lalitha, 8 hours after hysterectomy, two pads soaked in one hour, pulse 122, blood pressure 88/54, abdominal dressing dry. Please come now."

**What you write.**  
Operation name, pad count, vital signs, fluids, and surgeon called.

**Counter questions**

**144.1 She has severe abdominal pain and the abdomen is becoming hard, but the pad is only lightly stained. What do you do?**  
Call the surgeon anyway. Bleeding can be inside the abdomen. A hard abdomen, fast pulse, and dizziness matter as much as a soaked pad.

**144.2 She is shivering and the blood transfusion has just started. What do you do?**  
Stop the transfusion. Keep the vein open with normal saline on a new set. Call the doctor and the blood bank. This may be a transfusion reaction, not only the operation bleeding. Do not restart that bag.

### 145. Scenario: Strong labour pains start on the antenatal ward

**What you see.**  
Mrs Mary, a first-time mother at 39 weeks, is on the antenatal ward for high blood pressure that has been controlled. She now has regular pains every 3 minutes, lasting 45 seconds. She has a mucus show. The fetal heart is 138. She has a sudden urge to push.

**What you do first.**

1. Call the labour room and the doctor. Do not leave her alone if she is pushing.
2. Check the fetal heart. Take her blood pressure. A pushing woman with high blood pressure still needs that check.
3. If she is pushing already, do not move her in a wheelchair at speed without a nurse who can conduct a birth. Look. If the head is visible, prepare for birth on the ward and call for the delivery set. Support the perineum if the head crowns and help has not arrived. Note the time of birth if the baby comes.
4. If the head is not visible, transfer her to the labour room with a nurse, the file, and a wheelchair or trolley. Left side if she must wait.
5. Do not tell her to push hard before a trained person has confirmed full dilatation, unless the head is already crowning and birth is happening.
6. After any birth on the ward, dry the baby, check breathing, and watch the mother for bleeding. Give oxytocin for the third stage if that is your standing order.

**What you tell the doctor.**  
"Mrs Mary, 39 weeks, pains every 3 minutes, show present, fetal heart 138, she has an urge to push. We are transferring her now / the head is visible, please come now."

**What you write.**  
Contraction timing, fetal heart, blood pressure, whether the head was seen, transfer time, or birth time.

**Counter questions**

**145.1 She has fresh heavy bleeding with the pains. What changes?**  
Do not do a vaginal examination. Treat it as antepartum haemorrhage. Left side, oxygen, vein line, call the obstetrician, and do not send her walking.

**145.2 The bag of waters breaks during transfer and you see the cord. What do you do?**  
Stop the transfer position that presses the cord. Call for theatre. Keep a gloved hand lifting the head off the cord. Hips up. Do not push the cord back. This is the cord prolapse emergency.

<a id="10-one-page-revision"></a>

## 10. One-page revision

Say these numbers aloud the night before the interview.

### Adult vital signs

- Temperature: 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius.
- Pulse: 60 to 100 per minute.
- Breathing: 12 to 20 per minute.
- Blood pressure: about 120/80. Report 140/90 or more.
- Oxygen saturation: 95 to 100 percent in most adults. Urgent if below 90 percent.
- Urine: at least 30 millilitres per hour.
- Fetal heart: 110 to 160 per minute.
- Blood sugar low alert: below 70 milligrams per decilitre.
- Glasgow Coma Scale: best 15, worst 3, worry at 8 or below.

### CPR

- Adult compression: 100 to 120 per minute, depth 5 to 6 centimetres, 30 compressions then 2 breaths.
- Full recoil. Change compressor every 2 minutes. Pauses under 10 seconds.
- Child and infant, two rescuers: 15 to 2.
- Infant depth: about 4 centimetres, one third of the chest.
- Infant pulse: brachial. Adult pulse: carotid. Check for no more than 10 seconds.
- After an advanced airway: continuous compressions, one breath every 6 seconds.
- AED pad: upper right chest, and lower left side.
- Shockable rhythms: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.
- Adrenaline in arrest: 1 milligram every 3 to 5 minutes, on the team leader's order.
- Pregnant: push the uterus to the left, same compressions, call obstetric and newborn teams.
- Choking adult or child: 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts. Repeat. No abdominal thrusts in pregnancy, obesity, or an infant.
- Choking infant: 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts.
- If there is a pulse but no breathing: 1 breath every 6 seconds.
- Do not do a blind finger sweep.

### Hernia

- Indirect: through the deep ring, can reach the scrotum, younger patients.
- Direct: through Hesselbach's triangle, rarely in the scrotum, older men.
- Femoral: below and lateral to the pubic tubercle, more in women, strangulates easily.
- Inguinal: above and medial to the pubic tubercle.
- Strangulated: painful, tense, irreducible, no cough impulse, vomiting. Do not force it back.
- Operations: herniotomy in children, herniorrhaphy with stitches, hernioplasty with mesh.
- After surgery: watch urine, scrotum, wound, and chest. No heavy lift for about 4 to 6 weeks.

### Preeclampsia

- After 20 weeks: blood pressure 140/90 or more, plus protein in urine.
- Severe: 160/110 or more, headache, visual change, upper abdominal pain, brisk reflexes, low urine.
- Eclampsia: preeclampsia plus a fit.
- HELLP: haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets.
- Cure: delivery. Watch for 48 hours after birth as well.
- Pritchard loading: 4 grams slow intravenous, plus 5 grams intramuscular in each buttock.
- Maintenance: 5 grams intramuscular every 4 hours in alternate buttocks, for 24 hours after birth or last fit.
- Do not give the next dose if breathing is below 16, knee jerk is absent, or urine is below 30 millilitres per hour.
- Antidote: calcium gluconate 10 millilitres of 10 percent, slow intravenous.
- No methergine. No blood pressure tablets ending in pril or sartan.
- During a fit: left side, no spoon in the mouth, oxygen after the jerking, magnesium, check the fetal heart.

### PPH

- 500 millilitres after vaginal birth, 1000 millilitres after caesarean, or any loss with signs of shock. Also act at about 300 millilitres if the pulse or blood pressure is already abnormal.
- MOTIVE first response: Massage, Oxytocic drug, Tranexamic acid, Intravenous fluids, Examine the birth canal, Escalate care.
- Primary: within 24 hours. Secondary: after 24 hours up to about 6 weeks.
- Four Ts: Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin. Tone is the most common.
- Full bladder stops the uterus contracting.
- Soft uterus: massage and oxytocin. Hard uterus with bleeding: look for a tear.
- AMTSL: oxytocin 10 IU intramuscular, controlled cord traction by a skilled person, then massage.
- Oxytocin infusion for treatment: often 20 to 40 units in a bottle of salt fluid, such as Ringer's lactate or normal saline. No fast undiluted intravenous push.
- Misoprostol treatment: 800 micrograms.
- Methergine 0.2 milligrams intramuscular: not in high blood pressure or heart disease.
- Carboprost 250 micrograms intramuscular: not in asthma. Maximum often 8 doses.
- Tranexamic acid: 1 gram intravenous within 3 hours, plus the uterotonic. It does not replace oxytocin.
- First hour: pulse, blood pressure, uterus, and pad every 15 minutes.

### Labour

- True labour: regular pains, stronger and closer, cervix opens, show may be present.
- First stage: start of true labour to 10 centimetres. Latent to about 4 centimetres, then active.
- Second stage: full dilatation to birth of the baby. Do not push before full dilatation.
- Third stage: baby to placenta. Signs of separation: gush, cord lengthens, uterus firm and round and rises.
- Fourth stage: first 1 to 2 hours. Highest risk of bleeding.
- Placenta: check cotyledons, membranes, and two arteries plus one vein.
- Apgar at 1 and 5 minutes. Do not delay resuscitation to score it.
- Cord prolapse: hand lifts the presenting part, hips up, oxygen, stop oxytocin, urgent birth. Do not replace the cord.
- Shoulder stuck: call for help, McRoberts, no fundal pressure, no hard pulling on the head.
- Fresh bleeding before labour: no vaginal examination until placenta previa is excluded.

### Ward emergencies in one line

- Chest pain: stay, electrocardiogram, aspirin only if allowed. Do not walk the patient.
- One-sided weakness: note the onset time, nothing by mouth, do not give aspirin until a bleed is ruled out.
- Breathlessness: sit up. Do not lay a wheezing or frothing patient flat.
- Fit: side, nothing in the mouth. In pregnancy, treat as eclampsia.
- Sepsis: fever plus low blood pressure is a same-minute call.
- Fall: do not lift alone. Head injury on a blood thinner needs a doctor.
- Anaphylaxis: stop the drug, adrenaline 0.5 milligrams of 1 in 1000 into the thigh.
- Open abdomen with bowel showing: saline gauze, do not push the bowel back.
- Snake bite: no tight tourniquet, no cut, keep still. Dog bite: wash 15 minutes, vaccine not in the buttock.
- Late pregnancy bleeding: no vaginal examination.
- Missed period plus collapse and shoulder pain: think ruptured ectopic.
- Dry caesarean dressing can still hide internal bleeding.
- A mother who may harm herself or the baby is not left alone with the baby.

### Safety lines that impress the panel

- "I will stay with the patient and call for help."
- "I will check the name band, the allergy, and whether this medicine is unsafe for this patient before I give it."
- "I will not give methergine in preeclampsia."
- "I will not force a strangulated hernia back."
- "I will not put anything in the mouth during a fit."
- "I will record the time, the vital signs, the medicine, and the doctor's name."
- "If I am not signed off for the skill, I will call the person who is."

### Other numbers worth saying

- Autoclave: 121 degrees Celsius, 15 pounds per square inch, for 15 to 20 minutes.
- Adult adrenaline in anaphylaxis: 0.5 milligrams of 1 in 1000 into the outer thigh muscle.
- Drip rate: (millilitres × drop factor) ÷ minutes.
- Perineal tear: third degree includes the anal sphincter. Fourth degree opens into the rectum.
- Retained placenta: not out by 30 minutes of active management.
- Newborn vitamin K: often 1 milligram into the muscle for a term baby, as per protocol.
- Immunization at birth: BCG, oral polio zero dose, hepatitis B within 24 hours.

## A last word before you enter

Speak slowly. Define the word. Say what you do first. Say when you would call the doctor. If the examiner asks a counter question, answer that question only. Do not add five extra topics.

You are not expected to perform surgery or to choose a caesarean alone. You are expected to notice danger early, start the basic action, give the right report, and keep the patient safe until the doctor arrives.

Good luck. You have already done the hard part by studying with your own cases in mind. Use one real example from your ward if they ask, "Have you seen this?"
